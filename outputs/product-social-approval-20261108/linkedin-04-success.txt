											175 text In architecture school, I used to bring a whole building idea to a desk crit. In my head. I had so much to tell my studio professor. And almost nothing physical for them to react to. Years later, I learned there’s a name for part of this. Psychologists call it the illusion of explanatory depth: we feel we understand something until we try to explain it, or draw it, and the gaps show up. The head version cheats. The stair just works. The corner just resolves. It never has to pass a test. Paper doesn’t let you skip. Donald Schön, who studied how architects think, described design as a conversation: you make a move, and the drawing talks back. That’s why the first lines of a drawing always feel wrong. They’re the first honest answer the idea gets. An idea in your head can’t fail. Drawing it means trading perfect potential for something flawed and real. Back then, that felt like failing. Sometimes it still does. But it's actually the start of the design. I think about this far beyond design now. The email I haven’t sent. The concern I haven’t raised. The question I’m still polishing. The job role I haven't tried. If it lives only in my head, no one knows I’m thinking it. Make it exist first. You can make it good later. What’s sitting in your head right now that could start a conversation, if people could see it?
