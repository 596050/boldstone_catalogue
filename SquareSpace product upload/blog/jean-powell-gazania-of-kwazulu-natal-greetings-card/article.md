# A Flower That Tells the Time

A gazania will not open for a photographer on a dull morning, and it will not stay open once the sun drops behind a cloud. The flower is heliotropic in the most literal sense: the petals fan wide to face the sun as it moves and fold shut in low light, at dusk, and on overcast days, so that a gazania bed can look fully in bloom at midday and almost bare by four o'clock. Some species take the habit further and turn their leaves overnight so the woolly, paler underside faces upward, better placed to catch what little dew collects before dawn in a dry climate. Whoever photographed this particular flower was working, whether they thought about it that way or not, to the plant's own clock rather than their own.

Gazania belongs to South Africa, part of the same daisy family, Asteraceae, that gives the country so many of its best-known garden exports. Its common name, treasure flower, comes from the Latin gaza, meaning riches — a fair description of the colour concentrated in each bloom, typically orange or yellow banked with darker rings and flecks near the centre, arranged to guide a pollinating insect straight to the source of nectar. In the wild it grows low and tough along grassland margins and coastal sand, which is precisely the constitution that has made it useful everywhere else: gazanias tolerate salt spray, poor soil and long dry spells better than almost anything else grown for colour, which is why seaside planting schemes and motorway verges the world over lean on it so heavily.

The title carries a second name alongside the flower's own: Jean Powell, attached here to the KwaZulu-Natal garden where this particular bloom was found and photographed. The card does not explain further, and there is no need to invent what it does not say — the name simply marks a place, the way a photograph's caption sometimes names the garden rather than the road.

What the photograph is really interested in, beyond the flower's habit of clock-watching, is the concentration of colour and pattern packed into a single daisy head — the kind of close, patient looking that turns something common in a border into something worth stopping for. That is the habit running through this whole run of cards: not exotic subjects sought out for their rarity, but ordinary growing things looked at closely enough to reveal how deliberately they are built.

Sent as a note or kept as a small fixed image of a South African summer, the card asks nothing complicated of whoever receives it — only that they notice, as the camera did, how much structure sits inside a flower usually seen in a blur of colour from a moving car.
