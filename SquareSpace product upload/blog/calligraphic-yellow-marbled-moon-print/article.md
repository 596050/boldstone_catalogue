# Writing That Floats

Within the Turkish tradition of ebru marbling there is a specific branch called yazılı ebru, "written marbling", in which a marbler writes or draws a design directly onto the floating colour with a resist, most traditionally gall soap on the tip of a horsehair brush, before combing the rest of the bath into pattern around it. The resist repels the surrounding colour just enough that the written or drawn line survives the combing intact, sitting inside the finished marbled field like handwriting inside a wash of colour rather than on top of it. The loose, calligraphic lines that give this piece its name sit in that same tradition: marks made with the same floating, resisting logic as the swirled ground around them, rather than drawn afterwards.

The yellow running through the piece points toward one of the moon's most reliable optical tricks: the deep gold-yellow, sometimes almost orange, colour a full moon takes on low near the horizon, at moonrise or shortly after, compared with the pale white-grey it shows once it has climbed higher into the sky. The cause is the same atmospheric scattering responsible for a red sunset. Moonlight reaching the eye when the moon is low has to pass through a much greater thickness of atmosphere than moonlight arriving from overhead, and that longer path scatters out more of the shorter, blue wavelengths before they arrive, leaving the light that does get through skewed toward red and yellow. It is precisely why the year's most celebrated full moons, the harvest moon among them, are so often described and painted as yellow or gold: it is the moon closest to the horizon, at moonrise, that people are actually watching, not the same moon later in the night once it has climbed clear of the thick, low atmosphere and bleached back down to white.
