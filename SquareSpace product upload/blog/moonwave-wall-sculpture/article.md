# The moon that pulls the tide beneath it

Moonwave sets two forms in one field: a moon shape resting above, a wave folding below it, the two linked by shared shadow rather than by any literal join. At 74cm by 35cm, carved to a full 9cm in depth — deeper than most of the reliefs in this collection — it has more room than most pieces to let its concave shapes fall into real shadow rather than mere suggestion.

The pairing of moon and wave is not just a visual rhyme. It is physical fact: the Moon is the single largest force behind the rise and fall of the sea. Its gravity pulls at the water nearest it, raising a bulge on the side of the Earth facing the Moon and, because the Earth itself is also being pulled off-centre, a second bulge on the far side too — which is why most coastlines see two high tides and two low tides in roughly every day. The Sun pulls as well, more weakly because of its distance, but when Sun and Moon line up at new moon and full moon their pulls combine to produce the largest tides of the month, known as spring tides. When the Moon sits at a right angle to the Sun, at the quarter phases, the two pulls partly cancel and the tides are at their gentlest, called neap tides. None of this is visible to the eye standing on a beach — what you see is only the water moving — but the wave under the moon in this sculpture is, in a very literal sense, a wave the moon put there.

Carved by hand and then cast in white marble stone, Moonwave uses its extra depth to let the concave hollows beneath the moon form genuinely dark shadow rather than a light grey suggestion of one. Most of the smaller reliefs in this collection are carved to a shallower 5 or 6cm; at 9cm, Moonwave has enough undercut to hold real darkness in its hollows even under a fairly high, flat light, which makes it a more forgiving piece to place than some of the subtler works, though it still rewards raking light. Morning or evening sun picks out the ridge between moon and wave sharply; overhead daylight softens the whole piece into a gentler, more continuous roll of light and dark.

At 74cm wide and 35cm high, Moonwave sits comfortably as a single, elegant statement piece — wide enough to anchor a wall on its own, without needing a partner sculpture nearby. It suits an interior wall as easily as a garden wall, and because its shadow reads well even under moderate light, it is a reasonable choice for a spot that is not perfectly positioned to catch a raking sun, such as an entrance hall or a north-facing garden wall lit by a single evening spotlight. It sits well near other quiet, natural-form pieces without needing to compete with them.
