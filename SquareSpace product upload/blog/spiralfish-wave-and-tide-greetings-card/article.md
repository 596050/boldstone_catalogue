# The Fish That Swims Upright and Curls Its Tail Into a Spring

A seahorse breaks almost every rule a fish is supposed to follow. It swims upright rather than horizontally, propelled by a small fin on its back beating so fast it looks blurred even to the naked eye, and it has no scales at all, relying instead on a series of bony plates fused into rings beneath the skin. Strangest of all is the tail: rather than the flattened, paddle-like caudal fin nearly every other fish uses to drive itself forward, a seahorse has a long, tapering, fully prehensile tail that curls into a tight spiral, strong enough to grip a strand of seagrass or a piece of coral and hold on against a current the animal is otherwise far too weak a swimmer to fight.

That coiled tail is where the "spiral" in this card's title almost certainly comes from, and it is a genuine spiral rather than a decorative one — a helical grip built by muscle and bony ring working together, the animal's one real defence against being swept away from the sheltered seagrass beds and shallow reef margins it depends on for both food and camouflage. Seagrass meadows are themselves tidal, shallow habitats, rising and falling with exactly the rhythm the card's title names directly: wave and tide, the two forces a small, slow-swimming fish anchored by its tail has to negotiate every single day simply to stay in place.

Seahorses carry one more piece of natural history that tends to surprise people encountering it for the first time: it is the male that carries the eggs, in a dedicated brood pouch on the front of the tail, fertilised by the female during a courtship that can include synchronised colour changes and an elaborate shared dance. Whatever else the animal's odd, upright, armour-plated body is built for, it has clearly not been built along conventional fish lines at any stage of its life cycle.

A photograph that brings a seahorse's spiral tail together with the wave and tide of its shallow-water home is, in effect, showing cause and habitat in the same frame: the very thing that makes the seahorse vulnerable — a poor swimmer in open water — explained by the very thing that makes it well adapted to a specific, sheltered, tidal edge instead. It is a good example of how a single strange physical feature, looked at properly, tends to explain far more about an animal's whole way of life than its more obvious features do.

Sent as a note, it suits anyone with a fondness for the sea's odder residents, or simply anyone who appreciates a photograph that rewards a second, closer look once the initial shape has registered.
