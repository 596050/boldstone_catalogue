# Why an Eclipsed Moon Turns the Colour of Wine

Marbling is a technique of refusal. The pigments used in it are chosen and prepared specifically so that they will not dissolve into the liquid they are dropped onto, floating instead on the surface of a thickened bath, where a comb or stylus can drag them into pattern before the whole sheet of colour is lifted off, whole, onto paper. The stripes running through this piece are made with exactly that comb: a tool with regularly spaced teeth, pulled once through the floating colour, shearing rings or blobs of paint into long parallel bands in a single pass. It is one of the oldest patterning tools in the marbler's kit, and one of the most immediate, because the pattern appears at the speed of the hand moving, not built up gradually the way a brush stroke is.

The purple in the title points, deliberately or not, at one of the more genuinely strange sights the moon offers: the colour it turns during a total lunar eclipse. When the Earth passes directly between the sun and the moon, the moon does not vanish into black, as a simple shadow would suggest. It turns a dim, coppery red-purple, because Earth's atmosphere is still bending sunlight around the planet's edge and into the shadow, in exactly the way it bends sunlight at every sunrise and sunset. The atmosphere scatters blue light out of that bent beam far more readily than red, the same scattering that makes an ordinary sunset red, so only the reddish, longer wavelengths survive the journey through the full thickness of the atmosphere and reach the moon's surface. Every sunrise and sunset on Earth, all at once, is effectively being projected onto the moon during totality, which is why the exact shade varies from eclipse to eclipse: a clearer atmosphere gives a brighter copper-red, while high volcanic dust or heavy cloud around the Earth's rim can darken the eclipsed moon towards a deep, purplish grey, close to the tone running through this print.

Every piece in this series is floated and lifted individually, which is why the edition is limited to ten rather than open-ended: no two pulls from the bath produce quite the same pattern, so a limited run is a description of the process as much as a marketing decision. Printed onto 100% cotton rag paper, mounted on a high quality round window-cut mount board with a choice of mount colours, and backed with an acid-free card board, the piece arrives ready to be framed but without a frame supplied, which leaves that decision, sensibly, to whoever is hanging it.

Marbled pattern reads well under changing light, since the pooled and combed colour has a texture and a slight variation in tone that a flat, printed field would not, and it is worth hanging somewhere the light itself moves through the day: near a window rather than under a single fixed downlight. Glaze with UV-filtering glass regardless, since even printed inks fade under sustained direct sun. A round mount suits a grouping of several moons well, echoing the circular subject, but a single piece holds a small wall on its own without needing company.
