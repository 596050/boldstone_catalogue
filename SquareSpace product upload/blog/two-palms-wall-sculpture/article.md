# One structural idea, borrowed by ferns, palms and birds

Two Palms takes the sweeping curve of a palm frond as its subject, but the shape it captures is not unique to palms at all. A central spine with fine, repeated elements branching off along its length in matched, opposite pairs, a structure botanists and zoologists both call pinnate, turns up independently in palm fronds, in fern leaves, and in the flight feathers of a bird, three entirely unrelated lineages arriving at much the same solution. Two panels, each 50cm by 30cm, carved and cast in white marble stone, capture that curving, pinnate structure across a total span of 60cm.

A pinnate structure is, in engineering terms, a good way to build a large, light surface that still holds its shape. A single central rib, or rachis, carries the load and the plumbing, whether that is the vascular tissue feeding a frond or the hollow shaft of a feather, while much finer, repeated elements branch off to either side to create surface area without adding much weight. Ferns evolved this structure for their fronds long before palms existed; palms arrived at something very similar independently, arranging their leaflets along a central rib in a curve that grows more pronounced as the frond ages and its own weight pulls it downward; and birds, unrelated to either plant lineage, developed the same basic architecture in their flight feathers, a central shaft with vanes branching to either side, shaped to hold air rather than catch light. Three different kingdoms of life, solving three different problems, feeding, photosynthesising and flying, and arriving at a strikingly similar branching pattern each time. It is a clean example of what biologists call convergent evolution, where unrelated organisms independently evolve similar solutions because the underlying physical problem, building an efficient surface from a central support, only has a small number of good answers.

The two panels are carved by hand and then cast in white marble stone, the pinnate curve of the frond held with enough depth, at 7cm, to give real shadow along each fine rib. Because the sculpture is intended, in part, to work as a central piece lit from below, the carving is designed to hold its shape convincingly under an upward light source as well as ordinary daylight from above, the shadow falling in a different direction and giving the fronds a slightly different, more dramatic character after dark than they show in daytime.

At 50cm by 60cm across the pair, Two Palms suits a genuinely central position, such as a dining room wall or the end of a passageway, particularly where it can be lit from below to bring out the shift in shadow direction the piece is built to accommodate. Its curved, organic form offers a contrast to more rigidly geometric pieces nearby, without needing much width to make its presence felt.
