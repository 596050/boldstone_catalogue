# Calling an Onion Beautiful

The title is doing something almost provocative: it asks you to look at an onion, of all things, and accept the word beauty without irony. Nothing about the subject makes that easy. An onion is cheap, it is everywhere, it smells sharp enough to make you cry chopping it, and by the time it has started sprouting in a kitchen drawer it is generally considered past its best. Sally Price's watercolour disagrees, quietly and without argument, by simply giving the thing the same careful attention a painter would give a rose or a piece of jewellery.

That is, in fact, exactly what still-life painting has always done. When the genre came into its own in the Low Countries in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, its great subject was not grandeur but the kitchen table: fish still wet from the market, game hanging by the legs, bread, cheese, and above all vegetables, painted with a level of technical seriousness previously reserved for portraits of the wealthy. Part of the genre's whole argument was that ordinary, perishable, unglamorous things deserved the same discipline of looking that history painting gave to kings and saints. An onion, in that tradition, is not a lesser subject than a rose. It is simply a less obvious one, and choosing it says something about where you think beauty is actually located — not only in things already agreed to be beautiful, but in whatever rewards close enough attention.

Those same Dutch and Flemish still lifes often carried a second, quieter layer of meaning, particularly in the type known as vanitas: alongside the fine objects, painters would include something perishable or already turning — a wilting flower, an insect on a piece of fruit, a candle burnt low — as a reminder that everything on the table, however finely rendered, was on its way to decay. A sprouting onion sits unusually close to that tradition without needing to borrow its solemnity. It is simultaneously past its intended use and actively, visibly alive: not decaying so much as declining to stop growing, using its last reserves to push out new green growth rather than simply rotting quietly in the dark. It holds both directions at once — an ending and a beginning in the same object — which is precisely the kind of tension still-life painters have always gone looking for.

Watercolour suits that double reading well, because the medium itself is built from transparency and layering rather than flat, opaque colour — every wash sits on top of the last, visible through it, so the finished surface always carries a record of what came before. Painting an onion at exactly this stage, neither fresh nor finished, asks the same patience of the paint as of the subject: layer by layer, watching something changeable settle into its final form.

There is no need to argue the case further than the drawing itself does. Given close, patient, unhurried attention, almost anything in a kitchen turns out to reward it. An onion is simply an easy subject to underestimate, which makes it a good test of whether that principle actually holds.
