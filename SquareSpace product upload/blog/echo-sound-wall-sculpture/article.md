# Sound made visible, and slowed right down

Drop a stone in still water and the wave spreads outward as a set of perfect, expanding circles. Echo Sound borrows that same visual shorthand for a different kind of wave entirely: not water, but sound. Two panels, each 30cm square, carry concentric oval shapes across a sand-textured surface, together spanning 60cm — a wall sculpture that renders something usually invisible, a soundwave rippling outward and echoing back.

Sound itself travels as a wave of pressure moving through air, compressing and releasing molecules in a pattern that spreads outward from its source in expanding fronts, which is why concentric circles have become the standard shorthand for drawing sound, in comic books, in acoustic diagrams, and in exactly this kind of relief. An echo is what happens when that wave meets a hard surface and bounces back rather than being absorbed. Whether the human ear hears that reflection as a distinct echo or simply as part of the general reverberation of a room depends on timing: the brain needs a gap of roughly a tenth of a second between the original sound and its reflection to register them as two separate events, which in practice means the reflecting surface needs to be some seventeen metres away or more — a canyon wall, a distant cliff, an empty hall — for a true, separate echo to be heard at all. Closer than that, in an ordinary room, the reflections arrive too quickly to separate and simply thicken the sound instead. Some animals have turned this same physics into a primary sense: bats and toothed whales use echolocation, sending out calls and reading the returning echo to build a picture of what surrounds them in the dark or underwater, an entire sensory world built from exactly the kind of reflected wave this sculpture only suggests in still stone.

Echo Sound is carved with a fine, sand-like texture across each concentric ring, giving the surface a granular, light-scattering quality rather than a smooth, polished one. That texture matters for how the piece reads through the day: a sand-textured surface breaks up direct light into hundreds of tiny shadows rather than one clean line, so the rings appear to shimmer slightly rather than showing a hard edge, an effect closer to the soft, diffusing blur of an actual sound wave than a crisp line could ever manage. Cast in white marble stone after carving, the two panels are designed to be read together, the rings on one panel continuing the rhythm of the other.

At a modest 30cm x 60cm across the pair, Echo Sound suits a smaller wall or a considered spot within a larger scheme — a hallway, above a desk, grouped with other small pieces rather than needing to dominate a room. Because its texture depends on scattered light rather than one dramatic raking shadow, it is more forgiving of ordinary, general room lighting than some of the deeper, plainer reliefs in the collection, though it still rewards a lamp or spotlight positioned to catch the grain of the surface directly.
