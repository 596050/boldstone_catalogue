# The month, told in five stones

Moonphases Five lays out the Moon's changing face as a sequence: five square panels, each 30cm across, carved and cast in white marble stone, running to a full 150cm in width once hung with even gaps between them — long enough to read, at a glance, as the passage of time rather than a single fixed image.

The Moon does not produce its own light. What we see is sunlight reflecting off its surface, and the shape we call a phase is simply how much of that sunlit half happens to be facing Earth at a given moment, which changes constantly as the Moon travels around us. At new moon, it sits roughly between Earth and the Sun, its sunlit face turned away from us entirely, and it becomes invisible. As it moves along its orbit, a sliver of the sunlit side comes into view, a waxing crescent, thickening night by night through a first quarter, when exactly half the visible disc is lit, into a waxing gibbous, and finally to full moon, when it sits opposite the Sun in our sky and its entire near side is lit. The whole sequence then reverses, waning back down through gibbous, quarter and crescent to new moon again. One full cycle, known to astronomers as a synodic month, takes about twenty-nine and a half days, slightly longer than the Moon's actual orbital period, because the Earth itself is moving around the Sun at the same time, shifting the angle needed to complete the cycle. Human calendars have tracked this rhythm for as long as they have existed; the word month is directly descended from moon.

Each of the five panels in this sculpture is individually carved and then cast, the Moon's curved edge worked in relief so the play of light and shadow across the surface does real work: a crescent form depends on a fine, sharp line separating deep shadow from lit stone, while a fuller phase spreads that same light across much more of the panel. In this piece, the sculpture's own dependence on ambient light doubles the subject rather neatly — a relief showing the Moon is, like the Moon itself, entirely reliant on light arriving from elsewhere to be legible at all. As real daylight shifts through the day, or as an evening light is switched on, the five panels shift together, each phase reading slightly differently depending on where the light falls.

At 150cm wide, Moonphases Five is one of the more expansive single compositions in the collection, suited to a long wall with room to let the full sequence breathe — a dining room, a staircase wall, or a wide hallway. Because the piece depends on being read left to right, or right to left, as a sequence, the five panels need to be hung level, in order, and with even spacing, so the sense of one continuous cycle is not disrupted by an uneven gap or a panel out of place.
