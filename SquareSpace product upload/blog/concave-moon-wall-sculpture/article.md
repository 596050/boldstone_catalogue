# A dish that reads as a dome

Concave Moon plays a single, quiet trick, and it does it all day. The sculpture itself is a shallow dish carved into white marble stone, 50cm square — a moon-like disc that has been hollowed rather than raised. Look at it in the right light, though, and the eye insists it is doing the opposite: bulging out from the wall like a full, convex sphere. The piece does not change. Only the light does.

This is not a trick unique to sculpture. It is a well-documented quirk of human vision called shape-from-shading, and it rests on one deep assumption the brain makes about the world: that light falls from above. Given a rounded surface lit unevenly, the brain works backwards from the pattern of light and shadow to guess the shape, and it always guesses on the assumption that the light source sits above the object and the brighter side is closer to it. Turn a photograph of a real crater upside down and it will often look like a hill rather than a hole, because flipping the image also flips where the "top" of the light source appears to be, and the brain redraws the shape to match. A shallow, dish-like hollow, carved to catch light from one side just as a dome would, can trigger the same reversal: read as bulging outward, when in fact the stone curves inward. It is the same principle that lets an incuse coin design or a carved cameo hollow read as raised relief from a short distance, and it is the same principle, at planetary scale, behind why the Moon's own craters can look like domes in photographs taken at a low sun angle.

Concave Moon is carved by hand, then cast, and its whole effect depends on staying true to a single, directional light. In sunlight moving low across a wall, early or late in the day, the dish reads as convex, a quiet sphere pushing gently out from the stone. Move the light, or let the sun climb higher and flatten out, and the illusion softens, sometimes even flipping back to read as the hollow it actually is. It is the one sculpture in the collection where the changing light through the day does not just alter the mood of the piece — it can genuinely alter what shape you believe you are looking at.

Because the illusion depends on one clear direction of light, Concave Moon does best somewhere with a single dominant source — low winter sun through a west-facing window, an uplighter angled from one side, a single exterior spotlight on a garden wall at night — rather than a spot lit evenly from directly above or flanked by two competing lights, which would cancel the shadow gradient and flatten the effect. Given its modest 50cm square footprint, it works well as a single, contemplative object rather than needing a partner: a hallway, a stairwell landing, a garden wall with an evening light already in place.
