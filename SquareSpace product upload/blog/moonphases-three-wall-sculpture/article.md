# Three panels, one changing shape

Moonphases Three reduces the Moon's full cycle to its simplest possible telling: three square panels, each 30cm across, carved and cast in white marble stone, spanning 90cm once hung with even gaps between them. Where a longer sequence can show the Moon's gradual change in fine detail, three panels ask the eye to do more of the work, filling in the changes between one carved phase and the next.

The triptych, a composition built from three related panels, is one of the oldest and most durable formats in the history of art, long before it had anything to do with the Moon. Mediaeval and Renaissance altarpieces were frequently built this way, a central panel flanked by two others that could close over it like doors, and the format persisted precisely because three panels are enough to suggest a complete story, a beginning, a middle and an end, without needing the elaboration of a longer sequence. Three points are also, simply, the minimum needed to show that something is changing rather than static: two states can only show a before and an after, but three establish a direction, a sense of movement continuing past the frame. Moonphases Three uses that same minimal logic on an astronomical subject, each panel catching the Moon at a different stage, the gaps between them left for the eye to complete.

What the panels depict is real: the Moon's visible shape changes because we are seeing varying proportions of its sunlit half as it travels around the Earth over the course of roughly twenty-nine and a half days, a period astronomers call a synodic month. It is not the Moon itself changing shape, of course, but the angle between Earth, Moon and Sun, which determines how much of the lit hemisphere faces us at any given moment. A three-panel telling of that cycle necessarily compresses a month-long, continuous process into three still points, which is exactly what gives the piece its quiet, contemplative quality: each panel is a single held breath within a much longer, slower rhythm.

Carved by hand and then cast in white marble stone, each panel manages its own balance of light and shadow independently, since each represents the Moon at a different stage of illumination. As real light moves across the three through the day, the shadow within each carved phase shifts too, so a piece about the passage of time keeps enacting a smaller version of that same passage on its own surface, hour by hour.

At 30cm x 90cm across the three panels, Moonphases Three is a manageable, mid-sized piece, suited to a hallway, a bedroom wall, or a smaller sitting room where a full five-panel sequence would be too wide. The three panels should be hung level and evenly spaced, close enough that the eye reads them as one continuing idea rather than three separate small sculptures.
