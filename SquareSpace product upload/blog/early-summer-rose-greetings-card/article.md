# The First Flush, Not the Last

There is a particular moment in the rose calendar that this photograph catches, and it is not the one most associated with the flower in general memory — not the late, blowsy, overblown bloom of high summer, past its structural best and starting to drop petals, but the earlier one: the first full flush, when the year's growth has just produced its first complete flowers and the plant has not yet been through a full season of repeated blooming and deadheading. Early summer roses tend to be tighter, cleaner in form, and more intensely coloured than the same variety's later flushes, simply because they are new.

That timing is not incidental to how roses grow. Most modern garden roses are repeat, or remontant, varieties, meaning they are bred to flower more than once across a single growing season rather than producing one single flush and stopping, as many older rose types did. The first flush, arriving in early summer, draws on a full winter and spring's worth of stored energy and uninterrupted growth, and tends to be the most reliably abundant and uniform flowering the plant will produce all year. Later flushes, coming after the plant has already spent significant energy on that first display and often after some deadheading and pruning back, are usually lighter, sometimes patchier, and slower to open fully. A rose photographed in early summer is, in a fairly literal sense, the plant at its best-resourced and least depleted.

Breeding roses for exactly this kind of repeat, reliable flowering has occupied growers for a very long time. Old European roses, largely descended from species like Rosa gallica and Rosa damascena, mostly flowered once a year in a single, glorious but brief early-summer show. The introduction of Chinese roses carrying genes for repeat flowering into European breeding stock in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries changed what a garden rose could do entirely, eventually producing the hybrid tea roses and their many modern descendants that flower in successive waves from early summer through to autumn. The early summer flush this photograph captures is, in that sense, the moment the whole flower is arguably built around — the one earlier rose breeders worked with exclusively, and the one later breeding was designed to be able to repeat.

Photographing a rose at this specific point rewards close attention to form as much as colour: petals still tightly overlapped rather than loosening and reflexing outward, the centre of the bloom still partly closed rather than fully open and exposing its stamens, the whole flower holding a structural discipline it will gradually lose as the season goes on and the same plant produces looser, more relaxed blooms in its later flushes. It is a study of a flower caught at its most contained, rather than its most expansive.
