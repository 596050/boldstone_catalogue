# The Onion That Decided to Flower

An onion left in the ground, rather than lifted and eaten in its first year, has ambitions beyond the kitchen. Onions are biennial: the bulb a cook harvests after one growing season is really the plant's stored reserve for a second year it was never given the chance to reach, in which it would send up a tall, hollow flowering stalk called a scape, topped with a spherical cluster of small, star-shaped flowers known as an umbel. Cooks generally regard this event, when it happens early to a stored or planted bulb, as a problem rather than a milestone — bolting, as it is known, diverts the plant's energy into flowering at the expense of the bulb, leaving it woody and less useful in the pan. What looks like failure in a vegetable patch is, from the plant's own point of view, simply proceeding exactly as intended.

The trigger for early bolting is usually a spell of cold followed by warmth, a sequence called vernalisation that convinces the plant it has already passed through a winter and is due to flower, even when the calendar or the storage conditions involved were never part of any natural growing cycle. A bulb kept somewhere that swings from cold to warm — a shed, an unheated porch, the back of a cool cupboard through a change of season — can be tricked into exactly this response, sending up a flower bud from a bulb that was only ever meant to be food.

The flower itself, once it opens, is architecturally identical to the ornamental alliums grown deliberately in borders for their bold, spherical flowerheads — the same family, the same umbel structure, the same tightly packed cluster of small flowers opening from a single papery bud case at the top of the scape. Gardeners pay for that shape on purpose; a kitchen onion produces it by accident, and this card catches the moment just before the bud case splits, when the promise of the flower is visible but not yet delivered.

Watercolour and drawing together suit the bud's particular construction: a translucent outer sheath giving way to the tightly packed florets beneath, colour and structure that need both a soft wash and a defined line to read convincingly on paper.

As the last of four cards following one onion's unplanned second life, this is the point where the story stops being about storage and starts being about reproduction — the same plant that has spent long months quietly rooting and sprouting away in a dark cupboard, finally doing what it was always, underneath everything else, built to do.
