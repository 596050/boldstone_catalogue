# A Genus Named for the Rainbow

Iris takes its name from the Greek goddess of the rainbow, and it is one of the few plant names that undersells its subject rather than overselling it. The genus runs to more than two hundred species, spanning almost the full range of the visible spectrum between them, yellow, white, near-black, every shade of blue and violet, sometimes several colours combined on a single flower, and spanning an equally wide range of habitat, from dry, sun-baked Mediterranean hillsides to the wet margins of a pond. Wild iris tends toward the damper end of that range: many species are naturally at home at the edge of water or in damp meadow ground, sending up sword-shaped leaves in dense fans from a thick underground rhizome that stores the energy to do it again the following year.

This print shows the plant closer to that wild, undomesticated character than to the single perfect specimen bred and staked in a formal border. Rather than one isolated bloom, the photograph holds something closer to the plant's actual habit: the fan of narrow, blade-like leaves rising in clusters, flowers emerging at slightly different heights and angles from within that fan rather than lined up to face the camera. It is a more honest picture of how iris actually grows in the wild or in a naturalistic planting, in clumps, at slightly different stages of opening, competing gently with the leaves around them for light and space.

The leaves themselves are doing structural work the flower gets more credit for. Flattened, rigid, arranged edge-on to catch the sun with minimal surface area exposed to it, they overlap at the base in a fan that channels rainfall down toward the rhizome, an efficient piece of engineering that has nothing to do with the flower's colour and everything to do with the plant's actual survival, and that a photograph taken at this distance, rather than cropped tight to a single bloom, has room to show.

Blue and violet in the flowers comes from anthocyanin pigment, its precise shade shifting with the pH inside the plant's cells, which is part of why iris blue reads as a slightly unstable, living colour rather than a flat, printed one, deeper in shadow, lighter where the sun catches a petal directly. Photographed in the softer, broader light this composition needs, rather than the harder, more directional light a single macro bloom might want, that colour holds its depth across the whole image rather than concentrating in one small area.

Iris has been garden material for a very long time and remains one of the most reliable plants for difficult, damp ground where many other flowering perennials struggle, which is worth knowing if the print prompts any interest in growing the real thing rather than only looking at the photograph of it. As an image, it works as a slightly wilder, looser companion to a more formal botanical study: hang it near a tighter, single-bloom print and the contrast between the two, one controlled and isolated, one loose and habitual, does some of the composing for you.
