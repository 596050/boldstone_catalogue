# The Room Where Nothing Matched and Everything Belonged

Before museums organised their collections by species, period or region, wealthy collectors kept something closer to a private universe in a single room: the Wunderkammer, or cabinet of curiosities, a display cramped with whatever the owner found remarkable enough to keep. A narwhal tusk might sit beside a fossil, a carved shell beside a preserved beetle, a mineral specimen beside a piece of coral, grouped not by any scientific system but by the collector's own sense of wonder. The logic, to the extent there was one, was resemblance and contrast: things that rhymed in shape, or things that could not have been more different, placed close enough together that a visitor's eye had to do the work of comparing them.

That habit of grouping unrelated natural objects for the sake of what the eye finds between them did not vanish when museums professionalised their collections in the nineteenth century. It moved into illustration instead. A natural history plate showing several unrelated specimens on a single sheet — a fossil, a leaf, a shell, a feather, none of them from the same organism or even the same part of the world — is doing exactly what the old cabinet did, just on paper rather than on a shelf. The pleasure is the same one: noticing that a spiral in a shell and a spiral in a fern frond are, formally, close cousins, even though nothing else connects the two.

A drawing that earns the word "splendid" is one built from that same spirit of assembly — a page where the interest lies less in any single object than in the relationships a patient eye starts to notice once several are placed together. Graphite is well suited to this kind of grouping in a way colour sometimes is not, since tone rather than hue becomes the thing holding disparate objects together on the page; a shell and a seed pod rendered in the same range of greys start to belong to each other in a way they might not if one were shown in its true colour and the other in another.

This is, in its small way, a very old form of pleasure being offered on a very small object. The cabinet of curiosities existed to produce a particular kind of wonder — not the wonder of a single spectacular thing, but the quieter wonder of finding unexpected order in a room full of things that, on the face of it, had no business being together.

Sent as a note or kept on a shelf, the card rewards the same unhurried, comparing eye that a real cabinet of curiosities always asked of its visitors.
