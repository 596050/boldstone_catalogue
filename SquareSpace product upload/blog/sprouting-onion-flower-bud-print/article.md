# The Onion Nobody Lets Get This Far

An onion left long enough past sprouting will do something a kitchen rarely allows it time for: it will try to flower. This print, one of five watercolours following a single sprouting onion, catches that later, less familiar stage, the point at which the plant stops simply pushing up a leaf shoot and begins building toward a flower bud instead, a step most onions never reach because they are eaten, or compost, long before it happens.

Onions are biennial by nature, meaning their full life cycle is built to run across two growing seasons rather than one. In the first year, a bulb builds up its stored reserve of food underground and does no flowering at all. Only after passing through a cold period, an overwintering, does the plant switch into its second-year behaviour, called bolting, in which it sends up a tall, hollow flowering stem known as a scape from the centre of the bulb rather than another ordinary leaf. Onions belong to the genus Allium, alongside garlic, leeks and chives, and the whole genus shares a highly recognisable flower structure: a spherical cluster of many small individual flowers, called an umbel, borne at the top of the scape and initially wrapped in a thin, papery protective sheath called a spathe, which splits and peels back as the flower head matures and pushes out from inside it. That same spherical umbel, scaled up considerably, is exactly what makes ornamental alliums, close relatives of the kitchen onion, such a popular sight in borders and show gardens, their tall stems topped with a single dense globe of small flowers.

A flower bud stage caught on paper, rather than the fully opened umbel, is a genuinely less commonly observed part of the onion's life cycle, precisely because reaching it requires a bulb to be left alone, unharvested and untouched, for far longer than any kitchen use would allow. That makes this print less a study of a vegetable and more a study of a plant simply being permitted to complete a stage of its own biology that normally goes unseen.
