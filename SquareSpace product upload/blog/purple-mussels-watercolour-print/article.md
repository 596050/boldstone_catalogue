# The Colour Hidden on the Inside of a Shell

A mussel shell shows the world two quite different faces. From outside, it is dark, matt, almost forgettable, blue-black or brown and often dulled further by a rough coating of barnacles or algae picked up while the animal was alive. Opened, it reveals the opposite: a smooth, faintly iridescent lining that can shift through deep purples and blues depending on the angle of the light, the surface this watercolour study takes as its subject. The contrast between the two faces of the same shell is precisely what makes mussels a satisfying subject for close observational drawing rather than a passing glance.

That inner sheen is nacre, sometimes called mother-of-pearl, and it is built by the mussel itself, layer upon microscopic layer, from thin platelets of calcium carbonate glued together with a small amount of protein. The layers are thin enough, and stacked regularly enough, that light passing through them is split and reflected in a way that produces genuine structural colour rather than pigment colour, the sheen shifting slightly as the viewing angle changes, exactly as it does on the surface of a pearl, which forms by the same mechanism when a mollusc coats an irritant with the same nacre it uses to line its own shell. A mussel builds this lining as protection, a smooth, hard surface facing the soft body within, sealing off the rougher, more porous outer shell layer from the animal's own tissue.

Mussels attach themselves to rock, pilings or other mussels using a byssus, a bundle of tough protein threads spun from a gland in the foot and anchored to the surface almost like a tiny cable system, which is how a mussel bed can withstand being repeatedly pounded by waves at the shoreline without being swept away. They are filter feeders, drawing seawater across their gills to strain out plankton and organic particles, and a healthy mussel bed can process a substantial volume of water, making them a genuinely significant part of coastal ecology rather than simply a shape worth drawing.
