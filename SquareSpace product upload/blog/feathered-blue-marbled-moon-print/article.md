# Once in a Blue Moon

Feathering is a specific move in the marbler's vocabulary: after colour has been dropped and combed into a field of rings or waves, a fine stylus or rake is drawn back and forth across the surface in a light zigzag, pulling each ring out into a fine, tapering point, the way a comb dragged through wet hair leaves a feathered edge rather than a blunt one. It is usually the last thing done to a bath before the paper goes down, since the feathering is delicate and easily disturbed, and it turns a field of separate circles into something closer to a single, continuous, plumed texture, which is exactly the effect running through this piece's blue ground.

The phrase "once in a blue moon" is old enough that most people who use it have never wondered what it actually refers to. In modern usage it names the second full moon to fall within a single calendar month, an event that happens roughly once every two to three years simply because the lunar cycle, at about twenty-nine and a half days, is slightly shorter than most calendar months, so two full moons occasionally fit inside one. It has nothing to do with colour. Genuine blue-coloured moons are a real but much rarer phenomenon, and an entirely different mechanism: they need large quantities of dust or smoke particles of a very particular size, wider than the wavelength of red light but narrow enough to scatter it, suspended high in the atmosphere. Major volcanic eruptions have produced them; the 1883 eruption of Krakatoa is the most frequently cited example, reportedly turning the moon visibly blue or even green for people around the world for months afterward, as ash of just the right particle size scattered red wavelengths of moonlight out of the sky while letting blue pass through largely undisturbed, the reverse of the process that reddens an eclipsed moon.
