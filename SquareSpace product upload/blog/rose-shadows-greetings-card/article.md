# Not the Rose. The Shadow It Casts.

The obvious way to photograph a rose is to photograph the flower. This card is titled for the part almost every rose photograph leaves out: the shadow. Sally Price's image is built as much around what falls away from the bloom as around the bloom itself — the dark, curved shapes cast by overlapping petals, the soft-edged silhouette thrown across whatever surface sits behind or beneath the flower. The rose is present, but the shadow is doing at least half the composition's work.

A shadow like this depends entirely on how the object casting it handles light, and a rose is an unusually interesting object to test that with. Petals are thin enough to be partly translucent rather than fully opaque, particularly toward their outer edges where the tissue is at its most delicate, so the shadow a rose casts is rarely a single flat, solid shape the way a coin or a leaf's shadow tends to be. Instead it is layered and graduated: darkest where several petals overlap and block light completely, softening to a warm, faintly coloured gloom where only one thin layer of petal stands between the light source and the surface behind it. A rose's shadow, looked at closely, often tells you more about how many petals are stacked at any given point than the flower's own silhouette does from the front.

This interplay between transparency and shadow has occupied painters working with flowers for a very long time, well before photography existed to record it directly. Botanical illustrators historically had to solve the same problem by eye and by hand: how to suggest that a petal's edge lets some light through while its base, thickened and overlapped by its neighbours, blocks light entirely — a problem of translucency that watercolourists and engravers worked out through careful graduated washes and cross-hatching long before anyone could simply point a lens at a rose in low afternoon light and let the shadow fall where it would. Photography did not invent this interest in the interplay between a flower and the shadow it throws. It simply gave it a new, faster instrument.

Timing matters more here than with most subjects. Shadows this detailed and this legible generally depend on a single, low, directional light source — early or late in the day, when the sun sits close to the horizon and throws long, soft-edged shadows rather than the short, hard-edged ones of overhead midday sun. A rose photographed at noon casts barely any shadow worth studying. The same flower an hour before sunset can throw a shadow considerably more interesting than the bloom itself, which is presumably why this image exists at all: not because the flower needed recording again, but because the light, briefly, made something else worth looking at.

There is a useful discipline in that choice. A flower this familiar risks becoming background noise — everyone already knows roughly what a rose looks like. Turning the camera's attention to its shadow instead forces a slower, less automatic kind of looking, the sort that notices structure rather than simply confirming what was already expected.
