# The Papery Coat an Onion Sheds

An onion's outer covering has more in common with a shell than most people give it credit for. Dry, brittle, faintly translucent at the edges and marked with fine parallel ridges, the papery tunic wrapping a stored onion is not a separate skin at all but the remains of the bulb's oldest leaf bases, dead tissue left behind once the living layers beneath have taken over the job of storing food. Like a shell, it is armour built from what used to be living structure, now serving only to protect what is still alive inside it. And like a shell, once the occupant inside has outgrown or moved past what it protects, it splits, curls back, and is shed.

That is exactly the moment this piece catches: an onion held in storage long enough, or exposed to enough warmth and light, that dormancy has broken and a green shoot is forcing its way up through the crown, splitting the dry outer tunic as it goes. The contrast is the whole subject — brittle, papery, faded gold-brown coat against a shoot that is smooth, taut and unmistakably alive, pushing upward with a determination stored onions do not usually get credit for. It is a genuinely common kitchen sight, one most people register with mild irritation before throwing the bulb out, rarely as something worth sitting down to draw.

The tunic itself has a second life beyond protecting the bulb. Onion skins produce a strong, reliable yellow-to-rust-brown dye when simmered in water, a traditional method used for centuries to colour wool, fabric and, in various European Easter traditions, hard-boiled eggs wrapped in the papery layers before boiling. None of that history needs to be visible in a single sprouting bulb for it to be true — the same brittle covering that flakes uselessly into the kitchen bin has, in other hands, coloured cloth for generations.

Watercolour and drawing combined, as this piece uses both, suits the subject unusually well: a dry wash can carry the tunic's translucency and faint colour variation in a way flat colour cannot, while a firmer drawn line holds the crisp edge where paper-thin skin splits and curls back from the shoot beneath. It is a technical pairing that matches the subject's own combination of dead, papery covering and living green growth beneath it, one material for what has finished living, another for what has just started.

This card is one of a small set following a single humble kitchen onion through the stages of its unplanned second life — this one concerned with the coat it sheds rather than what grows beneath it.
