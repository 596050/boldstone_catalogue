# The fold that lets a swan fold away its wings

Swan Wing Folds takes its fan-shaped form from a specific, observable moment: the way a swan's wing gathers itself into pleats when the bird is at rest, each long flight feather sliding neatly under the next rather than lying flat in a single plane. Carved as a single panel, 70cm by 50cm, in white marble stone, the piece holds that folded, layered structure in relief, using the same shadow that makes the real feathers legible when a swan settles its wings.

Britain's resident swan, the mute swan, is named for being comparatively quiet as a caller among swans, but it is anything but silent in flight: its heavy wingbeats produce a loud, rhythmic, throbbing hum, audible at a genuine distance and distinctive enough that many birdwatchers can identify a mute swan overhead by sound alone, long before they see it. That same wing, folded at rest, is a small piece of good engineering. A bird's wing carries a long row of flight feathers, the primaries and secondaries, each one overlapping the next along its length rather like tiles on a roof or the leaves of a folding fan, so that when the wing is extended the whole surface locks together into one continuous, air-holding plane, and when it is folded, the same feathers gather and slide neatly against one another into a compact, pleated bundle tucked against the body. The fan-like folds this sculpture takes as its subject are, in other words, a real structural solution: a wing needs to open into a large, rigid surface for flight and close into a small, manageable shape for walking, swimming and resting, and the overlapping, layered feather arrangement is what lets it do both.

Swan Wing Folds is carved by hand and then cast in white marble stone, each fold kept distinct enough to read as an individual layer within the fan, echoing the way real overlapping feathers each catch the light slightly differently depending on their position in the stack. Through the day, a low, raking light picks out every individual fold along the fan with real clarity, while a higher, flatter light lets the folds merge into a softer, more continuous curve, closer to the impression a folded wing gives from a slight distance rather than examined feather by feather.

At 70cm by 50cm, Swan Wing Folds works well as a single anchor piece, suited to an interior wall or a garden wall where its organic, layered form can sit comfortably alongside other natural-history pieces without needing a partner sculpture of its own. Because its folds depend on raking light to read with real clarity, it benefits from a wall that catches some low sun or an angled lamp rather than only flat, even overhead lighting.
