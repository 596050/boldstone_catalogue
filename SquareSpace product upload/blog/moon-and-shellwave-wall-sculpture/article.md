# The spiral that water makes when it turns on itself

Moon and Shellwave sets a curved moon form beside a second shape: a wave caught at the instant it curls into a spiral, closer to a whirlpool's turn than to a shell's slow growth. Two panels, each 100cm by 22cm, carved and cast in white marble stone, together span 44cm in height, a quieter, more compact composition than some of the larger multi-part pieces in the collection, built from just these two linked ideas.

A wave curling into a spiral and a shell's coiled growth look similar, but they are made by two quite different processes. A shell's spiral, like an ammonite's or a nautilus's, is a record of biological growth: each new increment of shell is added at a constant rate relative to the animal's size, producing a smooth, ever-widening curve called a logarithmic spiral. Water curling into a spiral is doing something else entirely, obeying the physics of a vortex, in which a moving fluid, once it starts to rotate, tends to keep rotating around a central point because of the conservation of angular momentum, the same principle that makes a spinning ice skater speed up as she draws her arms in. A small whirlpool in a rock pool, the vortex that forms as bathwater drains, and a hurricane spiralling around its own eye are all, at wildly different scales, the same basic physical event: rotating fluid conserving its own momentum as it moves inward or outward. The curling wave in this sculpture belongs to that second family of spiral, motion caught mid-turn, rather than growth recorded layer by layer.

The two panels are carved by hand and then cast in white marble stone, the moon form kept as a simpler, calmer curve in contrast to the more energetic, tightening spiral of the wave beside it, so the two shapes read as a genuine pairing of opposites: stillness and motion, sharing a wall. Through the day, the tighter curves of the wave spiral gather more shadow into a smaller space than the broader curve of the moon, so the two elements shift at slightly different rates as the light moves, keeping the piece from ever settling into one static reading.

At 100cm wide per panel and 44cm tall as a pair, Moon and Shellwave suits a wall wide enough to give its horizontal format room, but modest enough in height to sit comfortably above furniture — a sideboard, a low bookcase, or a wide but not especially tall stretch of hallway. The two panels should be hung level and with a consistent gap so the moon and the spiralling wave read as one continuous composition.
