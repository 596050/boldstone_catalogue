# The Pull That Never Stops

A vein of colour running through a marbled field, the kind that gives this piece its name, is made by drawing a single tool through the bath in one continuous motion rather than combing evenly across it, letting the colour trail and thin unevenly the way a real current does around obstacles. Marbling has no way to erase or correct a stroke once it is made; the pattern exists only on the surface tension of the bath for the few seconds before the paper is lowered onto it, so a "river" of colour is quite literally a record of a single, uninterrupted gesture, not built up in layers the way a river's own bed is built up over years by moving water.

The moon's own relationship with water is more direct than a passing resemblance in a pattern. It is the moon's gravity, more than the sun's, that raises the tides in Earth's oceans, pulling a bulge of water toward it on the near side of the planet and, less intuitively, leaving a second bulge on the far side too, where the solid Earth is being pulled away from the water slightly more than the water itself is. As the Earth rotates through those two bulges roughly once a day, most coastlines experience two high tides and two low tides in every roughly twenty-four hour period. The sun adds a smaller pull of its own, reinforcing the moon's effect at full and new moon to produce the highest "spring" tides, and partly cancelling it at the moon's first and last quarter to produce smaller "neap" tides, a fortnightly rhythm that fishing and coastal communities have tracked for as long as they have depended on the sea, long before anyone understood gravity well enough to explain why it happened.

It suits a hallway or stairwell particularly well, spaces that are themselves about movement from one place to another, echoing the sense of flow in the print itself.
