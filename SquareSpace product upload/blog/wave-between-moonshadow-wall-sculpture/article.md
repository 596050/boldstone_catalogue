# The double ring that sometimes circles the Moon

Wave Between Moonshadow sets a spiralling wave at the centre of three panels, its curling form opening outward into two concentric rings, an effect the piece's own name compares to a halo around the Moon. Three panels, each 40cm square, carved and cast in white marble stone, run to a full 120cm across, the wave's spiral held between two suggested rings of shadow.

A halo around the Moon is a real, fairly common atmospheric phenomenon, and it has nothing to do with the Moon itself. High in the atmosphere, thin cirrostratus clouds are made up of countless tiny ice crystals, most of them shaped as hexagonal prisms, and when moonlight passes through a cloud of these crystals, it refracts, bending at a remarkably consistent angle because of the crystals' regular hexagonal geometry. The result is a ring of light around the Moon with a radius of almost exactly twenty-two degrees, roughly the span of an outstretched hand held at arm's length, visible on countless clear, high-cloud nights to anyone who happens to look up. Far more rarely, a second, larger and fainter ring appears at about forty-six degrees, caused by moonlight refracting through the same ice crystals at a different internal angle, producing a genuine double halo, two concentric rings of light nested around the Moon at once. It is this rarer, doubled version of an already delicate phenomenon that gives the piece its name, the spiralling wave at its centre standing in for the Moon itself, with two rings of implied shadow opening outward around it exactly as the two halos would.

The three panels are carved by hand and then cast in white marble stone, the central spiral held to a smooth, unbroken curve while the two outer rings are kept more restrained, closer to a suggestion of a ring than a fully modelled form, matching the faint, ghostly appearance of a real lunar halo rather than a hard-edged shape. That restraint asks a good deal of the light: a strong raking light draws the rings out clearly, while flatter, more even light lets them recede almost to invisibility, much as a real halo can vanish if the cloud thins even slightly.

At 120cm across the three panels, Wave Between Moonshadow is a substantial, wide piece, suited to a large wall with room to let the full sequence of spiral and rings breathe — a living room, a stairwell, or a garden wall seen from a reasonable distance. The three panels should be hung level and evenly spaced so the rings read as continuous and concentric rather than broken across visibly separate objects.
