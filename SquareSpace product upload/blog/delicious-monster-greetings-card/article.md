# Why the Leaf Has Holes

The plant behind both its odd common names, monster and delicious, is Monstera deliciosa, and this photograph is interested in the single feature that makes it instantly identifiable: the holes. A young Monstera leaf emerges whole, a plain green heart shape with no perforations at all. Only as the leaf matures does it begin to split and fenestrate, developing the irregular holes and deep lobes that give the mature plant its unmistakable, almost architectural silhouette. A single photograph capturing that fully fenestrated leaf is really capturing the end point of a transformation, not the plant's starting condition.

Why a leaf would evolve to grow holes in itself is a genuinely open question, and one botanists have proposed several competing answers to rather than settling on one. The leading theories all start from where the plant grows: in the understorey of Central American rainforest, climbing tree trunks toward patches of stronger light rather than standing in open sun. One idea holds that the holes let dappled sunlight fall through to leaves lower down the same plant, since a solid leaf would simply shade out everything beneath it, while a perforated one lets some light pass through to growth that would otherwise be starved. Another proposes that the gaps reduce wind resistance and physical damage during tropical storms, letting air pass through rather than tearing a solid leaf apart. A third suggests the irregular, hole-punched silhouette may make a mature leaf harder for a browsing animal to recognise as food at all, breaking up its outline against the dappled light of the forest floor. None of these has been established as the single correct answer, and it may simply be that the holes serve several of these purposes at once.

The plant's growth habit explains how it ends up high enough in a forest canopy for any of this to matter. Monstera deliciosa is a hemiepiphyte: it germinates on the forest floor but grows toward the darkest patch of ground it can detect, on the reasoning that deep shade usually marks the base of a large tree trunk, then climbs that trunk using aerial roots that grip the bark, hauling its own foliage steadily upward into better light rather than competing for space at ground level. The "deliciosa" in its name refers to the fruit it can eventually produce in the right conditions, a long, scaled, cone-like structure that develops a flavour compared to pineapple and banana once fully ripe, though the same fruit is unpleasantly irritating to eat before it has properly matured, owing to calcium oxalate crystals present through the unripe flesh.

Few houseplants have had a design moment quite as pronounced as this one. The bold, graphic silhouette of a fenestrated Monstera leaf became a fixture of mid-century modern interiors, and its leaf shape has been borrowed directly into textile and print design for decades, its confident holes and lobes lending themselves naturally to flat, simplified pattern work in a way that fussier foliage does not. It remains, decades on, one of the most reliably fashionable plants to photograph indoors, for reasons the leaf's own shape does most of the explaining for.

Photographing that leaf in strong, clean light lets the holes do exactly the compositional work described above in miniature: light falling through the gaps, breaking up what would otherwise be a single flat mass of green into a considerably more active shape.
