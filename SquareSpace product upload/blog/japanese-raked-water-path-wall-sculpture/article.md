# Lines drawn in gravel, held still in stone

In the dry gardens of Kyoto, a Buddhist monk draws the sea using nothing but gravel and a wooden rake. No water is present at all — the "sea" is suggested entirely by long, combed lines pulled through quartz gravel, sometimes circling around a placed rock the way real water eddies around an island. This four-part wall sculpture takes that raked line and does something the garden itself cannot: it holds the pattern permanently. Four panels, each 30cm square, carry the swirling line continuously across a total span of 120cm, carved and cast in white marble stone.

The tradition is called karesansui, usually translated as dry landscape gardening, and it developed within Zen Buddhist temple grounds in Japan, most famously at Ryoan-ji in Kyoto, where a small rectangle of raked gravel and a scatter of rocks has drawn quiet attention for centuries. The rocks stand in for mountains or islands; the raked gravel stands in for the sea or a river, its lines cut fresh with a rake after wind, rain or fallen leaves disturb the pattern. The garden is never finished. It is redrawn, by hand, again and again, which is part of the point — the raking itself is treated as a form of meditation, an exercise in attention repeated rather than a task completed once. What this sculpture borrows from that tradition is the line, not the impermanence: where a real karesansui garden must be raked afresh after every disturbance, the wall sculpture takes one moment of that line and fixes it, permanently, in carved stone.

Each of the four panels is carved by hand and then cast, the swirling grooves cut deep enough to hold a clear shadow along their length, echoing the way real raked gravel throws a fine line of shadow along each furrow when the sun is low. Because the pattern runs continuously across four separate panels rather than sitting within a single frame, alignment matters more here than in most of the collection — the four parts need to be hung level and evenly spaced so the eye reads one uninterrupted line moving across 120cm of wall, rather than four fragments that happen to resemble one another. Under raking light the grooves darken into a crisp, legible pattern; under flatter light they soften into more of a general texture, closer to the way a real gravel garden looks on an overcast day compared with a clear one.

At 30cm by 120cm across its full run, excluding the gaps between panels, this suits a long wall where a horizontal line of quiet pattern is wanted — a dining room, a corridor, a garden wall running alongside a path. Because each panel is a manageable 30cm square, the piece is also more flexible than some of the larger single-cast sculptures in the collection: the spacing between panels can be adjusted slightly to fit an awkward run of wall, provided the rhythm between them stays even enough for the line to read as continuous.
