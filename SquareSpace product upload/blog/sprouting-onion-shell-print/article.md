# The Papery Case an Onion Grows Inside

The onion's outer casing, the dry, crackling, faintly translucent layer that flakes away in the hand before you ever reach the part you cook with, is not skin in the way an apple has skin. It is called the tunic, and it is made of scale leaves that have already done their job and died back, left in place as a dry protective shell around the living bulb beneath. This print, one of a series of five watercolours following a single sprouting onion, looks closely at that outer case, the "shell" of its title, at the exact point where the papery brown tunic gives way to the pale, moist scale leaves still actively storing food underneath.

An onion bulb is, botanically, a short, flattened underground stem with a set of fleshy leaf bases, not roots or a swollen root as it is sometimes assumed to be, wrapped tightly around each other in concentric layers. As the outermost of those leaf bases finish their working life, they dry out, lose their moisture and colour, and turn into the thin, papery tunic that protects everything inside from drying out itself and from small pests and pathogens in the surrounding soil. That dry outer layer is also where much of the onion's brown pigmentation is concentrated, largely quercetin and related flavonoid compounds, the same broad family of plant chemicals responsible for a great deal of yellow, brown and red colouring across the plant kingdom, concentrated here in a thin, dead casing rather than in the living tissue it protects.

Watching a bulb sprout, as this five-part series does, means watching that relationship change: the dry tunic stays put and eventually splits or is pushed aside as the growing point beneath it drives upward, tearing a passage out through a case that was built to keep everything sealed in, not to let anything out. That small structural conflict, protective shell against emerging shoot, is a quietly dramatic thing to watch closely, and it is exactly the moment this print isolates.
