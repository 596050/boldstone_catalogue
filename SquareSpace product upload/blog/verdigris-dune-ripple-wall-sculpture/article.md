# What weather does to copper, what wind does to sand

Verdigris Dune Ripple pairs two natural processes in one small object: a form borrowed from sand dunes shaped by wind, and a colour borrowed from the slow chemical weathering of copper. Individually carved and cast at 35cm by 20cm, this piece carries a verdigris copper and rust finish rather than the white marble stone used elsewhere in the collection, an unusual and striking departure in colour for a collection otherwise built almost entirely from light and shadow on pale stone. Verdigris is not a pigment applied to a surface. It is what copper itself does when exposed to the world over time — a thin crust of copper compounds, usually carbonates, chlorides or acetates depending on the exact conditions, that forms on the metal's surface as it reacts slowly with moisture, air and whatever else it is exposed to. Left outdoors for long enough, almost any copper or bronze surface will develop it: the effect is famously visible on the Statue of Liberty, whose copper skin was originally a bright, penny-like brown when it was installed in the 1880s and has since oxidised, over decades of weather, into the pale green patina now inseparable from its appearance. Metalworkers have also long forced the same reaction deliberately, exposing copper to vinegar fumes or other mild acids to produce verdigris more quickly and with more control than weather alone allows. Either way, the blue-green and rust tones are a genuine record of chemical change over time, not a surface decoration, which gives a verdigris finish a certain kinship with the sand dune shapes it is carved into here, since a dune, too, is nothing but a record of slow, patient change, wind reworking the same grains of sand over and over until a shape emerges.

The dune ripple form beneath the verdigris finish is carved and cast in the usual way, but the colour is where this piece departs from the rest of the collection: rather than depending purely on white marble stone catching raking light and shadow, Verdigris Dune Ripple carries genuine colour variation across its surface, the copper and rust tones shifting subtly depending on the light, in a way pale stone cannot. It still behaves as a relief first, its ripple form shaped by carved undulation rather than paint, but the metallic surface adds a second layer of visual interest beyond light and shadow alone.

At a compact 35cm by 20cm, this is one of the smaller pieces in the collection, suited to a spot where its unusual colouring can be appreciated up close — a kitchen wall, a small study, or grouped with other smaller reliefs rather than needing to anchor a large room on its own.
