# A Fish Built Like a Mirror

A silver fish is not silver by pigment. Cut one open and there is no store of metallic dye anywhere in its tissue; the shine comes instead from stacks of microscopic guanine crystals arranged in the skin, layered like the sheets of a tiny mirror, reflecting light directly off the surface rather than absorbing some wavelengths and returning others the way a true colour does. It is structural colour, the same broad category of trick behind a peacock's blue and a butterfly's iridescence, and in a fish living in open water it serves an unusually precise defensive purpose: seen from below or the side, a mirror-scaled fish reflects whatever light and colour surrounds it, effectively taking on the colour of the water itself and becoming far harder for a predator to fix a clear outline on.

This is why so many fish that spend their lives in open midwater, rather than hiding among rocks or weed, converge on roughly the same silvery finish regardless of how distantly related they are to one another — herring, sardine, sprat, and, in British rivers and lakes, species like dace, roach and bleak all lean on the same structural trick, arrived at independently because open water offers nowhere to hide except inside the water's own reflected light. A fish that cannot hide behind an object instead learns to disappear into the medium it swims through.

Photographed as a single fish rather than as part of a shoal, the mirrored effect becomes something closer to a study in reflection than in identification — the scales catching and scattering whatever light was available at the moment the shutter opened, so that the fish reads, at a glance, as much like a moving piece of the water's surface as like an animal in its own right. That is very much the point of the adaptation: a silvery fish is not trying to look distinctive. It is trying to look like nothing at all, and photographed well, that attempt at invisibility becomes, paradoxically, the most striking thing about the image.

The vertical format suits the subject cleanly, giving a single fish room to be read top to bottom rather than competing for space in a wider, busier frame, and pairing well with a silver envelope that echoes the finish of the fish itself rather than simply matching a colour scheme. Shoaling species use the same silvering for a second, collective trick: hundreds of mirrored bodies flashing light at slightly different angles as they turn together can make a whole shoal harder for a predator to track as one target, each fish's flash blurring into the next.

Sent as a note, it suits an angler, a naturalist, or anyone drawn to the quieter kind of natural spectacle that depends on close attention rather than bright colour to make its case.
