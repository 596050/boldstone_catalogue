# The moon that doubles itself

Mirror Moons takes the single curved edge of a crescent moon and repeats it, reversed, so that two matching arcs face each other across a two-part wall sculpture — a moon and its own mirror image, set against the implied dark of the stone ground rather than an actual night sky. Each panel measures 100cm by 22cm, carved and cast in white marble stone, the pair spanning 44cm in height once mounted with a gap between them.

The idea of a mirrored moon is not only a compositional device. It happens, briefly and imperfectly, in nature. On genuinely still water, the Moon reflects as a single, sharp point of light, a true mirror image, just as a mirror on a wall would show it. The moment the surface is disturbed by even the slightest breeze, though, that single reflection breaks apart. Each small wave facet catches the moonlight at a very slightly different angle, and rather than seeing one point of light, the eye sees the sum of thousands of these tiny reflections, stretched into a long, glittering path running from the horizon to the shore — sailors have called this a moon path, or a moonglade, for as long as either word has existed. It is a genuine piece of optics rather than a figure of speech: a calm surface gives one true reflection; a broken surface gives a scattered field of light that the eye reassembles into a path. Mirror Moons plays with the calmer version of that idea, the single, clean, doubled image, rather than the shattered one — a moon reflecting itself with total fidelity.

The two panels are carved individually and then cast, kept identical in profile but installed as mirror images of one another, so the shared curve reads as a single symmetrical form split across two objects rather than two separate sculptures that happen to match. White marble stone gives the curve a soft, continuous gradient of shadow along its length rather than a hard single edge, which matters for the illusion of reflection: a true mirror image needs to feel continuous, without a visible seam, even though the two panels are physically distinct and hung with a gap between them. Through the day, as light moves across the wall, the shadow along both crescents shifts in tandem, keeping the two halves reading as one symmetrical idea rather than drifting out of step with each other.

At 100cm wide per panel and 44cm tall as a pair, Mirror Moons reads as a wide, low, horizontal form well suited to a wall above a sideboard, a headboard wall, or a long run above a fireplace, where its width can be appreciated without competing for height with anything else nearby. Because the symmetry depends on both panels being read together, they should be hung level and with even spacing, ideally on a wall long enough to give the whole composition some breathing space either side.
