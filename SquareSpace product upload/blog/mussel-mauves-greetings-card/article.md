# Shells, Emptied of the Sea

Where Boldstone's other mussel card shows the animal still attached to its rock, threads and all, Mussel Mauves shows the aftermath: shells lifted clear of the water, cleaned of grit, and set down to be looked at simply as objects. Sally Price's watercolour is not really about the mussel as a living creature here. It is about the shell as a found thing — the fan-shaped wedge of it, the deep mauve-to-violet gradient across the outer surface, and the paler, faintly glowing sheen of nacre where the shell has been turned to catch the light.

That inner sheen has its own explanation, and it is a close cousin of the science behind a beetle's iridescent back. Nacre, or mother-of-pearl, is built from many thousands of microscopically thin plates of aragonite, laid down in overlapping layers by the mollusc as it grows. Light entering those layers does not simply bounce off a single surface; it splits, reflects between plates, and recombines, producing the shifting, faintly rainbowed glow you get by tilting a shell slightly in the hand. It is structural colour again — an effect of physical layering rather than pigment — which is why nacre never looks quite the same colour twice, and why a single wash of paint can never quite pin it down. A careful watercolourist works around that by layering translucent washes rather than reaching for one flat mauve, letting some of the white paper show through in places the way light shows through the layered shell itself.

Shells like this have a long history as objects worth collecting rather than eating. Conchology — the serious, sometimes obsessive study and collection of shells — was a recognised pursuit by the eighteenth century, filling cabinets of curiosities alongside minerals and pressed plants. Sailors returning from long voyages brought shells home as souvenirs and sometimes worked them into elaborate arrangements under glass, a folk-art tradition that flourished in ports on both sides of the Atlantic. None of that history needs a mussel to be exotic. A common shoreline mussel, opened, cleaned and studied on a table rather than left on the rock, asks the same question any of those collected shells asked: what does this look like once you stop thinking about what it is for.

The mauve in the title is doing real work, because it is a difficult colour to get right. It sits between blue and violet, close enough to grey that it can collapse into mud if a wash is laid too heavy, and close enough to genuine violet that it can look artificial if it is overcorrected. Getting the true, slightly bruised mauve of a real mussel shell — not the brighter purple of an amethyst, not the flat blue-black of a photograph taken in poor light — is a matter of restraint: thin layers, patience, and a willingness to leave paper untouched rather than fill in every last patch of colour.
