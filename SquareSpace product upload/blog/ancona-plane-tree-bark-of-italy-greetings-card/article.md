# A Tree That Moults

Most bark stays where it is put. Plane tree bark does not. As the trunk thickens with each year's growth, the outer layer cannot stretch to keep up, so it splits and falls away in irregular plates, exposing paler, smoother bark beneath in patches of cream, olive and grey. The result is a trunk that looks, from a short distance, like military camouflage or a jigsaw missing its lines — no two trees patterned quite the same way, and no single tree patterned the same way twice, since the plates keep falling and reforming for as long as the tree lives. This card is a close photograph of exactly that surface, taken in Ancona, the Adriatic port city in Italy's Marche region where plane trees line piazzas and avenues the way they do across much of southern Europe.

The moulting habit is not decorative accident; it does real work for the tree. Rigid bark that could not shed would eventually crack under the pressure of the expanding trunk beneath it, and a tree that cannot manage its own girth is a tree in trouble. Shedding solves the problem cleanly, and it carries a second benefit that has made the plane family, and its close relative the London plane, famously tolerant of polluted city air: the flaking process physically removes soot, dust and grime that would otherwise clog the small breathing pores in the bark. During the coal-smoke decades of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, planted plane trees kept thriving in cities where other species suffocated, precisely because they were, in effect, cleaning themselves every year.

Little of that mechanism is visible to someone simply looking up at a plane tree in an Italian square, which is what makes a photograph like this useful. Pulled in close, the bark stops being background texture and becomes a genuine pattern in its own right — patches of colour fitted together with no repeating unit, the kind of organic, non-grid arrangement that has attracted painters and photographers to bark and lichen for as long as either medium has existed. It belongs to the same family of natural surface patterns Boldstone keeps returning to across its sculptural work: texture that only reveals its full structure once you stop looking at the whole tree and start looking at one square foot of it.

Plane trees have been planted along European streets and avenues for centuries specifically for their combination of fast growth, dense summer shade and resilience to hard urban conditions, which means a tree like the one photographed here has likely stood through a great many more summers of foot traffic, heat and pruning than most street furniture around it. The bark record keeps no precise diary, but its patchwork is, in a loose sense, a visible history of a tree constantly renewing its own surface.
