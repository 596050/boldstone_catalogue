# What an Orange Actually Is

Cut across the middle rather than peeled, an orange stops being a piece of fruit and becomes a diagram: a wheel of wedge-shaped segments packed around a central axis, each one bounded by a pale membrane, each one internally full of tightly bundled, fluid-filled sacs radiating out from the fruit's core. This 40cm watercolour and ink study, painted on 100% cotton rag paper in a limited edition of one, takes that structure as its subject, at a scale that lets the eye do the kind of close looking a piece of breakfast fruit rarely gets.

Botanically, an orange is what is known as a hesperidium, a specialised kind of berry unique to the citrus family. The outer rind has two distinct layers: the coloured, oil-rich flavedo on the surface, dotted with the tiny glands that release citrus oil when the peel is scored, and the white, spongy pith beneath it, called the albedo, which the fruit uses for structural support and which turns bitter with age. Inside the rind, the segments are technically carpels, each one a self-contained chamber walled in membrane and packed with juice vesicles, elongated cells that swell with liquid and give the fruit its characteristic burst of juice when bitten into rather than a smooth cut. The colour itself tells a seasonal story: a ripening orange is turning from green to orange not because it is manufacturing a new pigment from scratch but because chlorophyll, the pigment that made it green, is breaking down and revealing carotenoids that were present in the peel all along, the same class of pigment responsible for the colour of carrots and autumn leaves. Interestingly, in genuinely warm climates an orange can ripen fully and still stay partly green, since the chlorophyll breakdown that produces the orange colour is itself triggered by cool nights, which is why the fruit is dyed orange for some markets even once it is ready to eat.

Watercolour suits this kind of subject because it can hold translucency the way the fruit's own juice vesicles do, laying pale washes over one another to build the sense of a wet, light-filled interior rather than a flat, opaque disc. Ink then carries the structural information, the membrane lines separating each segment, the fine radiating pattern inside them, detail that would dissolve into a soft blur if it were painted in wash alone. Cotton rag paper holds both processes cleanly on the same sheet, taking a crisp ink line without bleeding while still accepting a soft, granulating wash beside it.
