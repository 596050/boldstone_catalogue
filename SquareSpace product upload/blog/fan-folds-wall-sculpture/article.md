# The shape that opens from a single point

Fan Folds takes its structure from one of the simplest and most versatile ideas in ornament: a set of curved lines radiating from a single point, exactly the way the ribs of a folding fan spread out from the rivet that holds them together. Carved as a single panel, 70cm by 50cm, in white marble stone, the piece keeps that same logic — every fold in the relief reads as part of one continuous unfolding movement rather than a repeated, unrelated pattern.

A folding hand fan is a small piece of practical geometry. Each rib is cut to the same angle, pivoting from one shared point, so that the whole fan opens into a section of a circle — wide enough to move air, compact enough to close flat. That same fanned, radiating structure, built from curved ribs meeting at a point, turns out to be one of the more useful shapes in architecture too, at a vastly larger scale. English Gothic builders of the late medieval period developed a distinctive style of ornamental ceiling known as fan vaulting, in which stone ribs curve outward from a springing point on the wall or column in a shape unmistakably like an open fan, multiplying and overlapping until the whole ceiling reads as a field of stone fans meeting edge to edge. It survives today in a handful of celebrated English buildings, among the best-known examples of a genuinely native contribution to Gothic architecture rather than one imported from the Continent. Fan Folds borrows that same underlying logic, if not literally its architecture: curved ribs unfolding from a shared origin, repeated until they read as a single coherent field.

The relief is carved by hand and then cast in white marble stone, each fold kept distinct enough to catch its own line of shadow while still reading as part of the whole. Because the folds curve rather than run straight, the shadow they cast changes character, not just length, as the light moves through the day — a low morning or evening light picks out the curve of each fold individually, while a higher, more direct light flattens the whole panel into a softer, more unified texture, the individual folds receding into a single rippling surface.

At 70cm by 50cm, Fan Folds works well as a single anchor piece on a wall that does not need a partner sculpture — a kitchen wall, a dining room, or a garden wall where its curved, radiating form offers a change of rhythm from anything strictly rectilinear nearby. Because its effect depends on shadow moving across curved rather than straight lines, it rewards a wall that gets some natural light through the day rather than one lit only by a single fixed downlight.
