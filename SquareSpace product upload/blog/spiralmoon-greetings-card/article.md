# The Curve That Shells and Galaxies Share

A seventeenth-century mathematician named Jacob Bernoulli became so fixed on one particular curve — the logarithmic spiral, which widens as it turns while keeping its proportions exactly the same at every scale — that he asked for it to be carved on his tombstone, along with the words spira mirabilis, the miraculous spiral. He got the sentiment right and the execution wrong: the mason carved an ordinary Archimedean spiral instead, one that widens at a constant rate rather than a constant ratio, missing the entire point of the request. It is a fitting mistake, because the two spirals look similar enough to fool most people, and only one of them is the shape that keeps appearing throughout the natural world — in nautilus shells, ram's horns, the seed head of a sunflower, and the arms of a spiral galaxy photographed from earth.

What makes the logarithmic spiral special is not decoration but growth. An animal building a shell by accretion, adding material only at the open edge, cannot resize the whole structure to keep up with its own growth — it can only add a bit more of the same curve. The logarithmic spiral is the one shape that lets an organism do this without the earlier, smaller sections becoming the wrong proportion for the animal living inside them. Efficiency produces the beauty, not the other way round, and that is generally true of most patterns in nature that people initially assume were arrived at for their looks alone.

The moon supplies a second kind of cycle rather than a spiral proper. Waxing from a sliver to a full disc and back again over twenty-nine and a half days, it repeats a shape sequence rather than a static form, one that has been used to track time by cultures with no contact with each other for as long as anyone has kept a calendar. Set beside a genuine spiral, the moon's own recurring arc reads as a kind of cousin: not the same mathematics, but the same instinct for something that grows, turns, or changes while still returning, eventually, to a form it has held before.
