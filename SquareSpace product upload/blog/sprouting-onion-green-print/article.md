# The Moment an Onion Starts Making Its Own Food

For most of its life, a stored onion bulb behaves like a bank account rather than a factory: it holds reserves built up the previous season and draws on them, but it does not generate anything new. This print, the last in a series of five watercolours following a single sprouting onion, catches the point at which that changes, the moment the pale, stretching shoot finally reaches daylight and turns green, switching the plant from spending its stored reserve to actively manufacturing new food of its own.

The green in question is chlorophyll, the pigment that captures light energy and makes photosynthesis possible, and a shoot growing in darkness, inside a cupboard or beneath the onion's own papery outer layers, does not produce it in any quantity. Deprived of light, the young shoot instead etiolates: it grows pale yellow-white and unusually elongated, channelling almost all of its limited energy into stretching upward as fast as possible rather than into the slower business of building chlorophyll and broader leaf tissue, a strategy that makes good sense for a shoot whose priority is escaping darkness before its stored reserves run out, not photosynthesising in conditions where there is no light to use anyway. Chlorophyll production in most plants is directly triggered by light exposure itself, through a light-sensitive precursor molecule that only completes its final conversion into working chlorophyll once it is struck by daylight, which is why a shoot can sit pale and thin for some time and then visibly green within a day or two of finally reaching a lit windowsill.

That switch marks a genuine change in the plant's whole strategy, not just its colour. A green, photosynthesising shoot is generating its own new energy for the first time, rather than only withdrawing from what the bulb stored the previous year, and it will typically thicken, slow its stretching growth and start producing proper leaf blades once that switch has happened, settling into the ordinary growth pattern of a rooted plant rather than the urgent, stretching sprint of a shoot still running on reserves in the dark.

The colour itself is worth a second look too, since the green that appears is rarely a single flat tone. An early shoot often shows a slightly yellower green near its base, closer to where light first reached it, against a fuller, more saturated green nearer the tip, a gradient that traces the shoot's own recent history the way growth rings trace a tree's. It is exactly the kind of gradual, close-observed shift that a watercolour study can hold far better than a quick glance at an onion left too long on a kitchen counter ever would, which is part of the point of painting it slowly and looking at it properly, side by side with the other four stages in the series.
