# Where an Onion Actually Begins

Everything below an onion bulb traces back to one small, unglamorous structure: the basal plate, a compressed disc of stem tissue no more than a few millimetres thick sitting at the base of the bulb, easy to miss entirely when the vegetable is whole and easy to overlook even when it is cut in half. This print, one of five watercolours following a single sprouting onion through its growth, gives that structure and the roots it produces the close attention they rarely receive, since most people meet an onion only after its roots have already been trimmed away in the kitchen.

The basal plate does two jobs at once, and both are essential rather than decorative. Every one of the onion's fleshy leaf-base layers, the part eaten as "onion", is attached to this single disc, radiating outward and upward from it the way pages fan out from the spine of a book. At the same time, the underside of the basal plate is where the plant produces its roots: a dense mat of thin, fibrous, adventitious roots, meaning roots that arise directly from stem tissue rather than from an earlier root system, as would be the case in most familiar garden plants. Those roots are shallow and spread wide rather than driving deep into the soil, an efficient strategy for a bulb that stores most of its own water and food reserve internally and mainly needs its roots for anchorage and for topping up moisture and minerals near the surface, rather than for the heavy long-term water and nutrient transport a tree's root system has to manage.

Watching a bulb sprout brings this normally hidden structure briefly into view in an unusual way: as the basal plate reactivates and pushes out fresh white roots in response to warmth and moisture, often before any green shoot appears above, it is frequently the first visible sign that a stored bulb has begun its new growing cycle, ahead of anything happening at the top. A close, patient drawing of that moment, roots emerging from a disc most people never look at directly, captures a genuinely underappreciated piece of everyday botany.
