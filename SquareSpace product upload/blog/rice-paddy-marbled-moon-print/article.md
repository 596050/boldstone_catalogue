# The Oldest Way of Painting With Water

Long before the Turkish ebru tradition that most people picture when they hear the word marbling, there was suminagashi, "ink floating", practised in Japan from at least the twelfth century. The technique is deceptively plain: drops of ink are placed on the still surface of plain water, and rather than dissolving they sit and spread into fine concentric rings, which the marbler then coaxes into pattern by blowing on them gently, fanning them, or touching the surface with a thread drawn through a bar of soap, whose surfactant pushes the ink outward from wherever it touches. A sheet of paper is lowered onto the surface, lifted, and the pattern transfers whole. It is a slower, quieter cousin of the more elaborate Turkish method that later spread into Europe, and closer in spirit to watching rain rings spread across a puddle than to painting in any conventional sense.

The rippled, terraced pattern in this piece takes its name from paddy fields, and the comparison holds up better than it might first seem. A rice paddy, viewed from above, is a landscape entirely reshaped by standing water: shallow, bunded terraces that follow the contour of the land, each one a thin skin of liquid holding a reflection of the sky, broken by the fine vertical lines of the rice itself. Rice cultivation across East and South Asia has for centuries been timed by the moon as much as the sun, lunar calendars marking the months for flooding, planting and harvest, and in Japan the rice harvest coincides with the autumn celebration of the full moon, when the year's most anticipated moon-viewing takes place specifically because a clear autumn sky, after the summer's humidity has broken, offers the best view of it in the whole year.

A patterned, textural piece like this one benefits from light that changes rather than a single static spotlight, since the terraced rings read differently in low, raking light than they do under flat, even daylight.
