# A Colour Leaves Are Not Supposed to Have

Green is the default colour of a leaf, so reliably that it barely registers as a colour at all, more a background condition of the plant world. Turquoise is not. A leaf rendered in blue-green rather than ordinary green reads as slightly wrong to the eye before the mind has worked out why, and that small friction is exactly what makes this 40cm watercolour and ink study, worked on 100% cotton rag paper in a limited edition of one, worth pausing on.

True blue and turquoise foliage does exist in nature, and it is rarer and stranger than it first appears. Most leaf colour comes from pigment: chlorophyll for green, carotenoids for the yellows and oranges that show once chlorophyll breaks down in autumn, anthocyanins for reds and purples. Blue is different. Genuine blue pigments are uncommon in plant tissue, and much of the blue or turquoise sheen seen on certain leaves, such as some begonias, peacock plants and a handful of tropical understorey species, is not pigment at all but structural colour: a very thin, precisely layered structure inside the leaf's outer cells that reflects blue wavelengths of light through interference, the same optical principle behind the colours of an oil film on water or a soap bubble. Plants that grow this structural blue sheen tend to live low down on a dense forest floor, in deep shade, and there is a working theory among plant scientists that the blue layer helps the leaf capture more of the limited red light reaching it by acting almost like a reflector behind the working chlorophyll, bouncing unused light back through the leaf for a second pass, not unlike how a cat's eye reflects light back through its retina. Whether or not that mechanism holds in every case, the visual effect is real and well documented: a leaf that shifts between green and an iridescent blue-turquoise depending on the angle it is viewed from, because the colour is being made by structure and light rather than fixed pigment.

Watercolour and ink are, again, a considered choice for this kind of subject, because structural colour itself shifts with angle and light in a way flat pigment never does, and a wash built from layered, slightly varying blues and greens can suggest that shimmer far better than a single flat blue-green mix would. Ink defines the leaf's veins and edge, the fixed architecture beneath the shifting colour. Cotton rag paper holds the fine ink work cleanly while still taking the granulating, slightly uneven wash that gives the colour its sense of movement.

The piece is mounted on a high quality round window-cut mount board, available with a choice of mount colours, and backed with an acid-free card board to protect the sheet long term.
