# Standing Tall: An Onion in Portrait

Most kitchen onions are drawn, if they are drawn at all, lying on their side: a dome of papery skin next to a knife, a study in curves. The Sprouting Onion Green Vertical card breaks that habit. Sally Price has turned the onion upright and let the card's tall portrait format do what a round bulb never could — follow a single shoot as it climbs off the top of the page. The eye starts at the base, where the bulb still holds its shape, and travels up the pale stem to the green blade at the tip, the only part of the whole composition reaching for something outside the frame.

An onion left in a rack or a drawer for too long does not simply rot. Inside the bulb, the plant has folded next year's growth into itself in advance — a stack of fleshy leaf bases, each one a ration of stored sugar and water, wrapped around a bud that has been sitting dormant since the bulb was lifted from the ground. Given enough warmth, that bud stops waiting. It does not need soil, and at first it does not even need light: it pushes straight up through the layers above it, drawing entirely on reserves the plant laid down the previous season. Because it is growing in the gloom of a cupboard rather than a sunlit bed, the shoot comes up pale and etiolated, reaching hard for wherever the light might be. That upward reach is pure geotropism — a plant's basic refusal to grow sideways when it can grow against gravity instead.

Botanical illustrators have always known that a plant like this wants a tall page, not a square one. The herbarium sheet — the pressed specimen mounted for a record book, a tradition going back centuries — is characteristically a narrow upright rectangle, because a plant's natural shape is rarely wide: root at the foot, a single stem up the middle, whatever leaves or flowers it carries arranged along that one vertical axis. A portrait-format card does the same job an herbarium sheet does. It gives a plant room to be read from the ground up, in the order it actually grew.

The medium matters here too. This is watercolour and drawing rather than photography, which means every gradation from the tight, striated bulb skin to the smooth, waxy green of the new shoot has been built by hand, layer over layer, in something like the way the onion itself is built in layers. Watercolour suits something translucent — a wash can be laid thin over the papery outer skin so a little of the white ground shows through, not unlike the way light passes through the onion's own tunic.

There is a reason this plain, stubborn vegetable keeps turning up as a subject rather than being left for the compost. Most still-life subjects are chosen and arranged. This one arranges itself. Nobody plants an onion in a drawer expecting it to grow — it does it anyway, on its own schedule, using only what it already had stored. A drawing that simply lets that happen, without embellishment, ends up being a study of persistence as much as of decay.
