# A Plant Older Than the Flower

A fern looks, at a glance, like any other leafy green plant — a frond, divided and subdivided into smaller leaflets, arranged in the kind of pattern that reads as instantly familiar. What that familiarity hides is how much older this particular way of being a plant actually is. Ferns belong to a lineage that was thriving on Earth long before the first flower ever opened, and a photograph that studies one closely is looking at a design that has needed remarkably little revision since long before flowering plants existed to compete with it.

The clearest sign of that older way of doing things is how a fern reproduces. It has no flowers, no fruit, no seeds. Instead, mature fronds carry tiny spore cases, often visible on the underside as neat rows or clusters of small raised dots, and each spore released is a single cell capable of growing, on its own, into an entirely separate and unfamiliar structure — a tiny, short-lived, flat green growth that itself produces the male and female cells which must find each other, usually via a film of water, before a new fern plant can begin. It is a two-stage life cycle with almost nothing in common with how a flowering plant reproduces, and it was already a fully working system hundreds of millions of years before pollen, petals, or seeds had evolved at all.

Before a frond reaches the flattened, familiar form a photograph like this one records, it goes through one of the more elegant pieces of engineering in the plant world: the fiddlehead, or crozier, a young frond tip coiled tightly in on itself like the scroll of a violin, which uncurls gradually as it grows rather than simply extending straight out of the bud the way most new leaves do. Botanists call this circinate vernation, and it lets a fern pack a surprisingly long, delicate frond into a small, protected coil while it is at its most vulnerable, only opening it out once growth is well underway. It is, in its own way, another of nature's spirals, tightly wound and slowly released.

Ferns have not always been treated with this much biological respect. In Victorian Britain, an enormous and genuinely strange popular craze for fern collecting swept through polite society, known at the time as pteridomania — fern fever. Sealed glass cases called Wardian cases, originally developed to transport live plants safely by sea, were repurposed as parlour ornaments to keep prized ferns alive indoors. Fern motifs turned up on ceramics, cast iron, textiles and book bindings, and amateur collectors combed the countryside for rare varieties with a single-mindedness usually reserved for far showier plants. It is an odd footnote in design history: one of the most understated, flowerless plants available managed, for a few decades, to become one of the most fashionable.

A photograph, rather than a drawing, records a fern's particular quality of green and its fine, repeating structure with a precision that suits the plant's own logic — each frond built from smaller versions of the same divided shape, repeating at a smaller scale the closer you look, a kind of natural fractal that rewards exactly the close attention a macro photograph can give it.
