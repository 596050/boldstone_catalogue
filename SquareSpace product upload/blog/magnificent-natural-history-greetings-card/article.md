# Small Enough to Miss, Large Enough to Notice

In 1665 Robert Hooke published Micrographia, a book of engravings showing ordinary small things — a flea, a piece of cork, the point of a needle — magnified far beyond what anyone had seen of them before. The flea alone, rendered at a scale that filled an entire fold-out page, reportedly startled readers so badly that some were said to have found it disturbing rather than merely interesting: an insect people swatted without a thought turned out, enlarged, to be armoured, bristled and considerably stranger than its size in life had ever suggested. Micrographia did not invent a new flea. It simply changed the scale at which one was allowed to be looked at, and changing the scale changed what counted as worth noticing altogether.

That trick — taking something routinely dismissed for being small and giving it the visual weight normally reserved for something impressive — sits behind a great deal of natural history illustration ever since, and it is a fair description of what a drawing needs to do to earn a word like "magnificent" honestly rather than as empty praise. Magnificence, in this tradition, rarely belongs to the largest or rarest specimen available. It belongs to whatever the artist has chosen to enlarge, literally or simply through the intensity of attention paid to it, until a viewer who would normally walk past the real thing without a glance is forced to stop.

Graphite is particularly suited to this kind of enlargement because the medium has no upper limit on the detail it can render, given enough patience: a soft pencil can hold the texture of a seed coat, the fine hatching of an insect's wing case, or the layered edge of a piece of bark at a level of finish that a quick photograph, taken in passing light, would never bother to capture. Drawing forces slowness onto both the maker and, eventually, the viewer, and slowness is the entire mechanism by which a small subject starts to look large. Nothing physically changes in scale. What changes is the amount of attention brought to bear on it, and attention is the one resource that can make an ordinary object magnificent without altering a single one of its actual dimensions.

There is a reason this habit of mind recurs constantly through natural history rather than being a one-off Victorian curiosity: it is simply true that most of the natural world worth knowing about is small, unglamorous and easy to overlook, and that the minority of showy, large, obviously spectacular subjects were never where the real interest was concentrated in the first place.
