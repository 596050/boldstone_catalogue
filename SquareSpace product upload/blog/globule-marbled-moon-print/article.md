# The Physics of a Drop That Will Not Sink

Drop ordinary ink into a glass of water and it billows downward, spreads, and disappears into solution within seconds. Drop marbling colour onto the surface of a prepared size bath and something different happens: the colour refuses to sink or dissolve. It spreads outward across the surface as a thin, floating skin, pushed by tiny differences in surface tension between the paint, its carrier, and the liquid beneath, an effect related to the same physics that makes a drop of washing-up liquid chase itself across a bowl of water, pushing weaker areas of surface tension outward ahead of it. Marblers add a wetting agent, traditionally ox gall, to the paint specifically to control how far and how evenly each drop spreads before it is touched by a comb or stylus, and every globule in this piece is a small, frozen record of that spreading, caught and lifted before it could spread any further or merge into its neighbours.

Globules of a different kind cover the moon's own surface, left there by the same basic event repeated for over four billion years: impact. A crater is essentially a splash, a globule in solid rock rather than liquid paint, formed in seconds when an incoming body strikes the surface at speeds of several kilometres a second, vaporising on impact and throwing a ring of shattered, molten and pulverised rock outward and upward before it falls back as a raised rim and a blanket of ejecta. The moon's brightest, most obviously young craters, Tycho chief among them, still show pale rays of ejected material streaking out across the surface for hundreds of kilometres, rays that have not yet been dulled by the slow darkening effect of billions of years of micrometeorite bombardment and solar wind, called space weathering. Without an atmosphere or water to erode them, and without plate tectonics to recycle the crust, the moon keeps its impact scars far longer than Earth does; nearly all of Earth's own equivalent craters have long since eroded away or been swallowed by moving continents.
