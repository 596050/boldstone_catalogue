# A Meadow Is Not a Lawn

A lawn is one species, cut short and often, bred for uniformity and for tolerating the mower. A meadow is the opposite of that idea: dozens of species of grass and wildflower growing together at different heights, flowering at different times through the season, left largely alone until a single cut at the end of summer once everything has had the chance to set seed. It is a much older way of managing land than the lawn, and a considerably more interesting one to look at, which is presumably why it is the subject of this print rather than the tidier alternative.

What a photograph of a meadow has to capture that a photograph of a single flower does not is texture at scale, not one bloom in isolation but a whole community of forms overlapping: seed heads catching the light at the top of the grass, a scatter of individual wildflowers pushing up through it at irregular intervals, stems bending very slightly under their own weight or the last of a breeze. There is no single subject for the eye to land on and stay with, the way there is with a close study of an iris or a rose. Instead the eye moves, the way it would moving through an actual field, picking out one seed head, then a flower, then a patch of grass gone gold with the season, then moving on again. That restlessness is the character of the image rather than a flaw in it.

Summer is the right season for this kind of density. British meadows peak in June and July, after spring bulbs and blossom have finished and before autumn cuts the growing season short, the point at which grass has reached full height and gone to seed, and the last of the season's wildflowers are still holding their colour before it fades. The light at that time of year tends to be warm and slightly hazy in the low evening hours, exactly the quality that turns a field of grass gold rather than simply green, and that warmth is what this print is built around.

Flower-rich meadows of this kind have become considerably less common across Britain than they once were, as more productive grass leys and more intensively managed farmland have replaced them over the course of the twentieth century, one of the quieter, slower environmental changes to the British countryside, and part of why a meadow left to grow and flower properly has become something closer to a deliberate choice than a default. That context is not necessary to enjoy the print, but it does explain why a meadow reads, to most people who encounter one now, as something gentler and more old-fashioned than the countryside usually looks.
