# A Ring Around the Moon, and What It Used to Mean

Marbling is often described loosely as painting on water, but the bath a marbler actually works on is not plain water at all. Plain water is too thin to hold a pattern; pigment dropped onto it would spread once and then slowly sink or diffuse into nothing. Marblers instead thicken the water first, traditionally with a mucilage made from carrageenan, a substance extracted from certain red seaweeds, or historically with gum tragacanth, until the liquid has just enough viscosity to let dropped colour float, spread to a controlled width, and hold whatever pattern a comb or stylus gives it for the short time before paper is laid onto the surface to lift the image away. The "water" in marbling is really a prepared, temporary skin, closer to a very thin jelly than to the water from a tap, and the quality of that size, more than almost anything else in the process, determines how cleanly a pattern will take.

Water and the moon have been linked in folk weather-reading for a very long time, most persistently through the "ring around the moon", a pale halo sometimes visible circling the moon on an otherwise clear night. Older weather sayings treat that ring as a promise of coming rain, and the folklore turns out to rest on genuine physics rather than superstition. The halo is caused by moonlight refracting through millions of tiny ice crystals suspended in a high, thin layer of cloud called cirrostratus, which reliably forms at the leading edge of an approaching warm front, well ahead of the heavier rain-bearing clouds behind it. Because those ice crystals are hexagonal prisms, they bend light at a highly consistent angle of twenty-two degrees, which is why the halo always appears at roughly the same apparent size regardless of the weather system producing it. Seeing the ring does not guarantee rain within hours, but it is a reasonably reliable sign that a weather front, and the wetter conditions that usually come with it, is on its way within a day or two, which is exactly the kind of practical, testable observation that old weather lore was built on before anyone had a forecast to check it against.
