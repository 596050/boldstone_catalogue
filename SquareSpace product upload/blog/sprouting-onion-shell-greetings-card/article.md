# The Onion's Armour, Held to the Light

Call it a shell and the onion's papery outer skin makes more sense than calling it anything else. It is dry, brittle, faintly rustling if you touch it, and its entire purpose is defensive — a wrapping the living bulb builds around itself and then, in effect, discards, letting it dry out completely while the moist, tightly packed layers underneath stay protected. This card studies exactly that outer covering: not the green shoot pushing through it, but the papery casing itself, at the point where it has thinned enough to let light pass through.

Botanically, that covering is made of tunics — the dried, flattened remains of the bulb's outermost leaf bases, which in life were fleshy and full of stored food in the same way the living layers beneath still are. As the bulb matures and cures, these outer layers lose their water entirely and collapse into thin, papery sheets, usually taking on a colour — russet, gold, deep copper, sometimes a purplish red — quite different from the pale flesh they once resembled. Their job, once dried, is purely protective: sealing moisture into the bulb below, keeping out soil pathogens and small pests, shielding the living tissue from bruising in transit and storage. An onion's shell, in other words, is a kind of armour the plant grows deliberately and then abandons to the weather, keeping its most vulnerable material safely behind a layer that is already dead.

What makes it worth painting is what happens to that same layer in strong light. Held up to a window, or against the sun, the dried tunic stops being opaque. It becomes a thin amber or copper-coloured screen, its fine internal fibres and faint growth striations suddenly visible as dark threads against a warm glow, closer to stained glass or a dried leaf than to anything you would ordinarily associate with a vegetable rack. That translucency is simply physics — the layer has become thin enough, in drying, for light to pass through the cell walls rather than bounce off them — but the effect reads as something closer to revelation: a covering built to conceal turns out, at the right angle of light, to show you almost everything.

Watercolour is a good medium for chasing that effect, because the technique itself depends on translucency. A wash laid thin lets the white of the paper show through exactly the way light shows through the onion skin, and a careful painter can use the same layering logic — colour built up gradually in overlapping glazes rather than mixed flat and applied once — to describe how the skin's own colour deepens where it folds over itself and thins where it stretches tight. The subject and the medium are, for once, doing the same thing by the same method.

There is also something quietly apt in choosing to study the outer skin rather than the shoot beneath it. Most attention naturally goes to the growing tip, the visible sign of life continuing. The shell gets noticed only when it is about to be peeled off and thrown away. Giving it a careful, patient look before that happens is simply paying attention to the part of the story that is usually skipped.
