# The fountain frozen into geometry

Deco Fountain draws on a subject Art Deco returned to again and again: water caught not as a splash but as a fan of rising, radiating lines, symmetrical and controlled. This single-panel wall sculpture is 100cm by 50cm, carved and cast by hand in white marble stone, its relief kept deliberately subtle — closer to a whisper of texture than a deep-cut pattern — so the fountain form emerges gradually rather than announcing itself.

When the term "Art Deco" was coined, it borrowed its name from a single exhibition: the Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs et Industriels Modernes, held in Paris in 1925, a vast showcase of the new decorative style then spreading through architecture, furniture, jewellery and film sets across Europe and America. Fountains were a natural subject for the movement to adopt. A fountain is water behaving geometrically already — rising in a column, spreading in an even arc, falling in parallel streams — so it needed very little translation into the fan-shaped, radiating motifs that Deco designers favoured on everything from cinema façades to cutlery. Unlike a wave or a shell, which had to be simplified to fit the style, a fountain arrived pre-stylised: symmetrical, rhythmic, already close to ornament. That is likely why the motif turns up so often in the period's public architecture and applied design, wherever a designer wanted to suggest abundance and movement without resorting to a literal, splashing picture of water.

Deco Fountain keeps its relief shallow, and that restraint is deliberate. A subtler cut means the sculpture depends even more on the angle of the light than a deep relief would; carved this gently, the fountain's radiating lines can vanish almost entirely under flat, even light and then resolve sharply the moment the light comes from one side. White marble stone was chosen for the way it holds that soft gradation — a flatter or glossier surface would either wash out the subtlety altogether or turn it brittle and hard-edged, neither of which suits a subject built from the idea of gently rising water. Watch it across a day and the fountain seems to change speed: harsh cross-light in the early morning or evening makes the radiating lines snap into focus, while the flatter light of midday lets them recede, almost to stillness.

Because the relief is subtle rather than deep, Deco Fountain rewards a wall where it will actually receive some raking light — a window at an angle, or a picture light mounted above it — rather than a corridor lit only from directly in front. At 100cm wide and 50cm high it reads comfortably as a single statement piece rather than needing a partner, which makes it a natural choice for a wall that needs one considered object rather than a grouping: above a console table, in a stairwell, or on an interior or garden wall where its quieter register will not compete with anything nearby.
