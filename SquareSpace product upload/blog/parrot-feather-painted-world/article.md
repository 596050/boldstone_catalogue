# The Colour No Other Bird Can Make

Painted close and large, at a scale no living bird offers the naked eye, a single feather fills this 40cm square: the central shaft running true down the middle, the barbs combing away from it in even ranks, the whole thing held inside a round window cut through its mount board. Watercolour and ink on 100% cotton rag paper, edition of one, this piece belongs to Boldstone's Painted World series, and it rewards the kind of close looking that a feather rarely gets when it is still attached to a flying bird.

A feather's structure is more engineered than it looks. The central shaft, or rachis, carries rows of barbs branching off at an angle, and each barb in turn carries rows of barbules, microscopic hooks that zip neighbouring barbs together into a single flat, air-tight vane, the way a zip fastener locks two rows of teeth into one continuous edge. Preen a feather the wrong way and it separates into loose fronds; preen it back into place and the barbules re-hook themselves, which is why a bird can rough up its feathers to trap warm air and then smooth them flat again in seconds. Colour is where parrots do something genuinely unusual among birds. Most bright feather colour is either pigment (absorbing some wavelengths of light and reflecting others) or structure (scattering light off a nanoscale arrangement of keratin so that only certain wavelengths bounce back, the same physical trick that makes the sky blue). Parrots use both, and for the reds and yellows they rely on a family of pigments called psittacofulvins, found in no other order of birds. Their greens are frequently not a green pigment at all but a blue structural colour showing through a thin wash of yellow psittacofulvin sitting above it, two entirely different colour-making mechanisms stacked in a single barb.

Rendering that in watercolour and ink asks the paint to do two jobs the feather itself does. Ink lays down the fine, disciplined linework of the shaft and the ranked barbs, the kind of regular structure that would blur into mud if it were painted as wash alone. Watercolour then carries the colour, thin and layered the way the feather's own barbules are layered, translucent enough that light still seems to move through it rather than sit on top. Cotton rag paper is what allows both to happen on the same sheet: a fine, hard-sized surface that lets ink hold a crisp line without feathering, while still accepting the softer bloom of a watercolour wash beside it.
