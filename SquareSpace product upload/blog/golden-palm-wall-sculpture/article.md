The palm frond has carried symbolic weight for a very long time, in more than one tradition at once. In ancient Greece and Rome, a palm branch was awarded as a mark of victory, presented to the winners of athletic contests, and the phrase palm of victory survives in English long after the actual practice has faded from memory. The same frond carries a related meaning in Christian tradition, remembered in Palm Sunday and, in early Christian funerary art, carved onto tombs as a symbol of triumph over death. Ancient Egyptian visual language used the palm rib differently again: a notched palm branch served as a hieroglyphic sign associated with the passage of a year, its regular notches read as the steady, countable rhythm of time itself, and by extension linked with the idea of a long life or a long reign. Three unrelated traditions, then, converging on the same plant for broadly related reasons, longevity, triumph, and the marking of time, each reading its own meaning into the frond's confident, radiating shape. That accumulated symbolism found a further life much later, when early twentieth-century designers returned to the palm frond as a favourite decorative motif of their own, prizing its confident symmetry rather than any of the older meanings attached to it.

Golden Palm is carved in the same way as the collection's stone reliefs before being finished in gold leaf rather than left as bare white marble stone. That reflective surface changes how the piece behaves entirely: rather than a soft shadow gradually shifting across a textured surface through the day, gold leaf catches and throws back a sharper, more localised glint of light, brightening and dimming with even small movements of a lamp or candle rather than the slow arc of the sun. The peaceful, symmetrical curve of the palm form suits that glimmering treatment, giving the piece a quiet, warm presence in the kind of low, close indoor light gold leaf is built to catch.

At 50cm by 30cm, Golden Palm is a modestly scaled piece well suited to a dining room, a bedroom, or any spot where it will be seen mostly under artificial light in the evening rather than direct daylight, since gold leaf performs at its best under exactly that kind of warm, changeable indoor lighting. Because it is compact and self-contained, it also travels well within a room, sitting as comfortably above a mantelpiece or a chest of drawers as it would on a larger, more prominent wall.
