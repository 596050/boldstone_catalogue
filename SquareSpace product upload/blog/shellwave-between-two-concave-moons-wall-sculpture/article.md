# A wave curling into a shell, held between two moons

Shellwave Between Two Concave Moons arranges three ideas along one wall: a moon shape, a wave curling into a shell-like scroll, and a second moon shape, in a three-part sculpture that reads left to right as a single composition. Each panel measures 100cm by 22cm, carved and cast in white marble stone, the three together spanning 66cm in height, one of the larger and more considered compositions in the collection.

The shell-like curl at the centre of this piece is not just a decorative flourish. It describes something a wave actually does in its final moments before breaking. As a wave moves into shallowing water, its base slows against the rising seabed while its crest keeps moving at speed, and the top of the wave begins to outrun its own base. On a steeply shelving beach, this produces what surfers and oceanographers call a plunging breaker: the crest arcs forward and down ahead of the rest of the wave, curling over into a hollow tube before it collapses, a shape that, for the brief moment it holds, looks remarkably like a scroll or an open shell. Gentler, more gradually sloping beaches tend to produce the softer, foaming spill of a spilling breaker instead, without that dramatic curl. The scroll shape captured here belongs to the more dramatic version of that process, the moment of maximum curl just before the wave gives way.

The two moon forms bookending the wave carry a quieter idea of their own. Carved as shallow, dish-like concave shapes, they depend on the direction of the light to decide whether they read as hollowed out or gently domed, the same trick of shape-from-shading that lets a concave surface, lit consistently from one side, appear to bulge outward rather than recede. Framing a curling wave between two shapes that themselves shift with the light doubles the piece's dependence on where and how it is lit.

All three panels are carved by hand and then cast in white marble stone, the central wave held to a more pronounced curve than the flanking moons, so the eye reads a clear sequence: calm, curling, calm. Through the day, the wave's deeper curve throws the most dramatic shadow of the three, darkening and lengthening as the light rakes across it, while the two moons shift more subtly between their hollow and domed readings depending on the same light.

At 100cm wide per panel and 66cm tall as a group, this is a substantial, horizontally arranged piece suited to a large wall with room either side — above a long sideboard, in a wide hallway, or on a garden wall where its full run can be appreciated without crowding. The three panels need to be hung level and evenly spaced so the sequence reads as one continuous idea rather than three separate objects.
