# Counting the Spirals on a Pineapple

A pineapple in full portrait, base to crown, is not a difficult subject to recognise, but it is a surprisingly difficult one to draw honestly. The skin is not smooth like an apple's, nor striped like a melon's. It is tiled — a tight mosaic of diamond-shaped facets running in curved bands from the base up to the spiked crown — and Sally Price's watercolour takes on that whole tiled surface rather than simplifying it into a general impression of "pineapple". The tall format holds the fruit the way it actually grows: squat base, tapering body, a crown of stiff leaves reaching past the top edge of the card.

Those diamond facets are the reason a pineapple rewards close looking more than most fruit. Each one is not decoration; it is the outer face of a single flower. A pineapple plant produces a dense spike of a hundred or more small flowers packed tightly together, and as they mature and swell they fuse into one another, leaving the fruit we eat as a single mass built from many individual former flowers — botanists call this kind of compound structure a sorosis. Every hexagonal facet on the skin marks where one of those flowers used to be.

What makes the pattern genuinely worth stopping for is the way those facets are arranged. They do not run in straight vertical or horizontal lines. They spiral, in two directions at once, curving one way in one set of rows and the opposite way in another. Count how many spiral rows run in each direction and you consistently land on two numbers from the Fibonacci sequence — most commonly 8 rows one way and 13 the other, sometimes 13 and 21. This is not a coincidence particular to pineapples. The same spiralling arithmetic governs how seeds pack into a sunflower head and how scales wind around a pine cone: a growth pattern that adds each new element at a fixed angle to the last, which happens to be the most efficient way to pack a great many similar parts into a confined, expanding space. A pineapple is that mathematics wearing fruit as a disguise.

Rendering that in watercolour and drawing is a discipline in restraint as much as observation. Every facet has to sit at a slightly different angle to its neighbours to describe the fruit's curved body, and the ripening colour — green at the crown end, warming to gold toward the base, since a pineapple ripens from the base upward — has to shift gradually enough that the tiling still reads as one continuous skin rather than a patchwork. The crown of leaves at the top, stiff and radiating, is really a rosette of its own: cut it off a ripe fruit, let the base callous over, and it will root into a new plant, as anyone who has tried it on a kitchen windowsill will know.

The pineapple carries an odd weight in design history for a fruit native to South America. It arrived in Europe as an exotic, hard-won luxury — difficult to grow, harder to transport — and became, by the eighteenth century, a fixed symbol of hospitality and status: carved in stone on gateposts, gilded onto ceiling plasterwork, set as finials on gables to tell visitors they were welcome. A watercolour that studies the fruit closely, rather than using it as shorthand for "tropical" or "luxury", works against that old decorative habit, looking at the actual structure rather than the symbol it became.
