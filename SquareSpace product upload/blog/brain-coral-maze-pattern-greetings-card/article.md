# A Reef Animal Shaped Like a Thought

The resemblance is not a trick of the light or a generous imagination. Grooved brain coral, Diploria labyrinthiformis, grows into a domed mass covered in continuous winding ridges and valleys that genuinely do look like the folded surface of a brain, closely enough that the coral's scientific name simply means "maze-shaped" and its common name has never needed improving on. The valleys run five to ten millimetres wide, cut a few millimetres deep, and wind across the whole surface of the colony without repeating a straight line for any distance — the same restless, unbroken quality that makes a real maze, or a real brain, difficult to summarise in a single glance.

What looks like one creature is, in fact, a great many. Each groove is a shared home for a row of coral polyps, small soft-bodied animals living side by side, tentacles ringing a shared mouth that runs the length of the valley rather than each polyp holding a separate one. The whole colony grows by these polyps slowly secreting calcium carbonate beneath themselves, building the ridged skeleton upward a little at a time — and slowly is not a figure of speech here. Grooved brain coral adds roughly three and a half millimetres of height a year, which sounds negligible until it is set against a colony recorded at close to two metres across, implying a single coral head old enough to have been growing, generation of polyps after generation of polyps, for several centuries. Few animals of any kind live that long as a continuous, identifiable individual.

The maze pattern itself is not there for effect. More ridge and valley means more surface area packed into the same overall footprint, which means more room for polyps to catch drifting food and more surface for the coral's symbiotic algae, living in the tissue and photosynthesising on the coral's behalf, to gather light. Efficient packing, again, produces a shape that happens to fascinate anyone who looks closely at it — much the same logic that shapes a sycamore seed's wing or an agave's spiralling leaves, just built from limestone instead of leaf or wing.

Photographed close enough to fill the frame, as this card does, the coral loses its context as a reef animal entirely and becomes almost abstract: a labyrinth with no beginning or end visible, cream and buff ridges catching light unevenly across a surface that never quite repeats itself. It rewards the same close, patient looking that runs through the rest of this range of cards — a reminder that some of the most intricate patterns available to look at were never built by an artist at all.
