# A Colour Beetles Rarely Wear

Pink is not a colour the beetle world reaches for very often. Open any illustrated guide to British or European beetles and the palette runs overwhelmingly to black, brown, and the metallic register — bottle green, gunmetal blue, bronze, the deep oil-slick sheen of a ground beetle's back. Pink turns up rarely enough that a beetle wearing it stands out from the page before you have worked out what species it even is. Sally Price's watercolour puts a single specimen close to the paper's edge, filling most of the frame, so there is nowhere for the eye to go except into that unlikely colour and the architecture underneath it.

Most of what looks like "colour" on a beetle's back is not pigment at all. A beetle's hardened forewings — the elytra, the stiff case that folds down over the delicate flight wings beneath and gives beetles their characteristic shell-like look — are built from layers of chitin and protein only a few microns thick. In many species those layers are stacked with enough precision that they behave like a diffraction grating: light bounces between the layers and interferes with itself, cancelling some wavelengths and reinforcing others, in exactly the way a soap film or an oil slick produces colour with no pigment involved. This is structural colour, and it explains why so many beetles look metallic — that particular sheen is a physical effect of layered, near-transparent surfaces, not a dye. Pigment-based colour, by contrast, comes from actual chemical compounds absorbing and reflecting specific wavelengths regardless of viewing angle, and tends to look flatter and more stable under changing light. A pink beetle is more likely working with pigment, in the carotenoid family most insects can only obtain from what they eat, which is one reason the colour reads as unusual rather than merely rare: it is doing something structurally different from the shimmer beetles are best known for.

This distinction is not just a technical footnote. It is the reason beetles have been a favourite subject for natural history illustrators for as long as the discipline has existed. Before colour photography, and for a long time afterward, watercolour was the only reliable way to record what a beetle's colour actually did — because structural colour shifts with the angle of the light and the angle of view, a single photograph can flatten an iridescent green beetle into a dull black smear if the light catches it wrong. An illustrator working from a specimen in hand, turning it under a lamp, can choose the single truest angle and fix it, giving the viewer a settled, legible version of a colour that in life never stops moving. Entomological illustration is, in that sense, an argument that some colours need a human eye and a steady hand more than they need a camera.

Watercolour also suits the beetle's particular kind of surface: a hard, glossy case rather than the softer matte textures of fur or leaf. Getting that glassy hardness to read on paper means working with sharp highlights against saturated colour, rather than the gradual blending a furred animal would need — a different set of decisions from the softer washes a petal or a shell might ask for elsewhere in the same range of cards.
