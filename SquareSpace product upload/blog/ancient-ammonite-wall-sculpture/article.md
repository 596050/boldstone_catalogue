# The spiral the sea kept for 400 million years

Ancient Ammonite is the largest single subject in this collection, and it earns the scale: two panels, each 120cm by 60cm, carved and cast by hand in white marble stone, together spanning a full 120cm by 120cm on the wall. The subject is a coiled shell, its chambers spiralling outward in a smooth, mathematically consistent curve — the same recognisable form that turns up in fossil collections, in geology textbooks, and, cut into rock along England's south coast, in the cliffs themselves.

Ammonites were marine cephalopods, part of the same broad family of animals that includes today's squid, octopus and cuttlefish, and their closest living relative in body plan is the nautilus, which still carries a coiled shell not unlike theirs. An ammonite grew its shell in a single continuous spiral, adding new, larger chambers as it grew and sealing off the old ones behind walls called septa; where those septa met the outer shell wall, they left intricate, folding lines called sutures, often the most beautiful part of a well-preserved fossil. The spiral itself is what mathematicians call a logarithmic, or equiangular, spiral: a curve that expands by a constant factor with every turn, so that the shape of the whole shell is, in effect, the same shape at every scale, just larger. It is the same family of curve found in a nautilus shell, in the arrangement of seeds in a sunflower head, and, at a much greater scale, in the arms of a spiral galaxy — a curve that keeps recurring in nature because it is what results whenever something grows by adding material at a constant rate relative to its own size.

Ammonites first appear in the fossil record hundreds of millions of years ago and did not survive the mass extinction event at the end of the Cretaceous period, roughly 66 million years ago, the same event that ended the age of the non-avian dinosaurs. In the time between, they were extraordinarily successful and diverse, with thousands of species ranging from a few centimetres across to well over a metre — and because different species evolved and disappeared relatively quickly while spreading across wide areas of ancient sea, geologists still use particular ammonite species today as index fossils, markers that let them date the rock layers in which they are found with real precision. England's Jurassic Coast, running along Dorset and Devon, is one of the world's well-known sources of ammonite fossils, eroding steadily out of cliffs that were themselves laid down as sediment on the floor of a warm Jurassic sea. The name itself is older than palaeontology: writing in the first century, the Roman naturalist Pliny the Elder described fossil ammonites as resembling the curled horns of the Egyptian god Ammon, and the name endured.
