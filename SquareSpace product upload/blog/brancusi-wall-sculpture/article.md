# The totem pole, reduced to its essential line

Brancusi takes its name and its logic directly from a specific reference: the totemic wooden carvings of Constantin Brâncuși, the Romanian-born sculptor who spent most of his working life in Paris and became one of the twentieth century's most influential pioneers of abstraction in sculpture. Two panels, each 50cm by 25cm, carved and cast in white marble stone, together forming a 50cm square, borrow the reduced, stacked, geometric vocabulary he developed rather than any single one of his works.

Brâncuși trained in the conventional, highly detailed academic sculpture tradition of the late nineteenth century, and then spent much of his career deliberately moving away from it, paring his subjects down to their simplest possible geometric or organic form: a bird reduced to one continuous soaring curve, a head reduced to an egg-like oval, a kiss reduced to two interlocking blocks. Alongside his work in bronze and polished metal, he carved directly in wood, and here he drew heavily on the folk-art traditions of his native Romania, where carved wooden gateposts, grave markers and totemic columns used simple, repeated geometric units — stacked lozenges, chevrons and rings — to build a tall, patterned form from modest, legible parts. His best-known work in this vein, the Endless Column, takes exactly that logic and repeats a single carved unit upward in a rhythmic, modular sequence, standing today as a public war memorial in Romania. What connects that folk tradition to this sculpture is the same underlying idea: a satisfying form built from a small number of simple, honestly repeated shapes, rather than from elaborate surface detail.

The two panels of this sculpture are carved by hand and then cast, in white marble stone, kept to a full 8cm depth, deep enough to give each stacked, geometric unit real weight and shadow rather than a shallow, decorative surface pattern. That depth suits the totemic reference: Brâncuși's carved wooden columns depended on the physical thickness of the wood to give each carved unit real sculptural presence, and this piece does the same in stone. Through the day, the deep carving throws strong, changeable shadow along each stacked form, the piece reading as a more solid, architectural presence in low, raking light and a flatter, more graphic pattern under direct overhead light.

At 50cm square across the two panels, Brancusi is a compact but weighty piece, well suited to a hallway, a study, or alongside other sculptural objects where its stacked, totemic form can be appreciated at a considered height rather than from a distance.
