# The Leaf That Predates the Flower

The ginkgo leaf is one of the most recognisable shapes in the plant world, and also one of the strangest, once you stop taking it for granted. It is a simple fan, split by a shallow notch at the outer edge into two rounded lobes, with none of the netted, branching veins that run through almost every other leaf you could put next to it. This design, by Jennifer Evelyn Tomlinson-Price of Evelyn Jenni, takes that fan shape at its single showiest moment of the year — the uniform, unbroken gold the tree turns to just before it drops the lot in what is often only a day or two.

That unusual vein pattern is not a stylistic quirk. Ginkgo biloba belongs to a lineage of plants, the Ginkgoales, that split off from other trees long before flowering plants existed at all — commonly described as a living fossil because leaf impressions found in rock many tens of millions of years old are barely distinguishable from the leaves on a street tree today. Where a maple or an oak leaf carries a branching, netted network of veins, a ginkgo leaf's veins fork cleanly in two from the base outward, over and over, never merging back together — a pattern botanists call dichotomous venation, and one that is essentially unique among trees still living. Looking closely at a single leaf, in other words, is looking at a piece of design logic that predates almost every other tree shape familiar in a British street or park.

Ginkgo is also dioecious — individual trees are either male or female, never both — and it is only the female trees that produce the notoriously strong-smelling fruit, which is one reason so many street and park plantings favour male cultivars exclusively. What the tree is best loved for, though, is the autumn performance: rather than the gradual, patchy colouring most deciduous trees go through, ginkgo tends to hold its leaves resolutely green for weeks and then turn the entire canopy to a single, even gold almost overnight, before dropping nearly the whole crown within a day or so of the first hard frost. Where most autumn colour is a slow negotiation, ginkgo autumn is closer to a switch being thrown.

Its resilience is part of the story too. Ginkgo tolerates urban pollution, compacted soil and root disturbance better than most trees its size, which is why it has become such a common choice for city avenues and temple grounds across Asia and, increasingly, streets and parks in Britain — a tree that manages to be both ancient beyond almost any comparison and thoroughly at home in the modern city.

A fan shape this clean rewards exactly the kind of flat, graphic treatment given to it here — bold outline, confident colour, none of the fussy detail a more conventionally veined leaf might demand. It is a design that trusts the leaf's own shape to do the work, rather than layering on unnecessary ornament.
