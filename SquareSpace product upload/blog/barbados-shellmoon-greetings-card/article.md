# What the Moon Leaves on the Sand

Every shell that ever washed up on a Barbados beach arrived on a schedule set two hundred and thirty-eight thousand miles away. Tides are the moon's doing: its gravity pulls a bulge of ocean toward it as the earth turns beneath, and that bulge, arriving twice a day on any given shoreline, is the mechanism that strands shells, weed and driftwood at the high-water mark for beachcombers to find. When the sun lines up with the moon at new and full moon, their combined pull produces the highest of the high tides, spring tides, which reach further up a beach and leave a richer scatter behind them than an ordinary day's tide manages. A card that puts a shell and the moon together is not being poetic for its own sake. It is naming a genuine cause and effect.

The shell itself carries its own quieter connection to the same idea of repeating cycles. Grown by accretion, a small amount of shell material added at the growing edge with each cycle of the animal's life, most spiralling shells follow a logarithmic curve — a shape that widens as it turns but never changes its proportions, so that a young shell and a mature one of the same species look like scaled copies of each other rather than different shapes entirely. It is the same mathematical curve that turns up in ram's horns, in the cross-section of a nautilus, and, at a scale that has nothing to do with biology, in the arms of a spiral galaxy. None of that requires the shell to have anything literally to do with the moon. It simply means two of the oldest visual motifs available to an artist — the spiral and the crescent — sit naturally together, because both describe things that grow or change by turning.

Barbados sits at the eastern edge of the Caribbean, its beaches built from coral sand rather than the volcanic black sand of some of its neighbours, and its shallows support the kind of shell life — conches, cowries, tritons among them — that has made Caribbean beachcombing its own small tradition for visitors and residents alike. A photograph taken there, pairing a single shell with a night sky, is working with material that is genuinely local as well as generally symbolic.

Card and image work together well here: a square format gives a shell and a moon equal weight rather than forcing one to dominate, and the pale ground of the printed card keeps the contrast close to what the original photograph would have shown against a dark sky.
