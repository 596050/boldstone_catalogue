# The Moth That Eats Lichen, Not Leaves

Most moth caterpillars in Britain eat leaves, which is unremarkable enough that it barely needs stating. One small, pale grey-green moth breaks the pattern entirely: its caterpillar feeds on lichen, the slow-growing partnership of fungus and alga that crusts old bark, stone walls and gravestones, an odd and mineral-poor diet almost no other member of its family bothers with. The adult moth carries the marks of that unusual choice on its wings — a fine, irregular tracery of darker lines crossing a pale, faintly mossy ground, a pattern so close to the texture of lichen-covered bark that the moth all but disappears the moment it settles on the surface it was raised on.

That wing pattern is what has earned moths of this general type their popular "lace" nicknames, the fine crossing lines reading, at a glance, like the fine ground of a piece of lace held up to the light. It is camouflage first and ornament only by consequence: a moth resting by day on a lichen-crusted branch needs to disappear from the birds that would otherwise pick it off before dusk, and a broken, irregular pattern does that job far better than any solid colour could, breaking up the moth's outline rather than simply matching a single background shade. Look closely enough, though, and the same pattern that hides the moth from a bird is exactly what makes it worth drawing once it has been found.

This drawing calls the pattern "shadows," which is an accurate way of describing what graphite does with it. A photograph would record the moth's colouring as colour; a pencil has no colour to offer at all, only tone, so the artist is forced to translate every marking on the wing into some value of grey between white paper and black lead. That constraint turns out to suit this particular subject unusually well, since the moth's own camouflage was never really about colour either — it is built from contrast and interruption, light patch against dark line, exactly the vocabulary graphite speaks most naturally. A drawing of a lace-winged moth is, in a sense, working in the same currency the moth itself evolved to work in.

There is a pleasing closed loop running through the whole subject: a moth that eats lichen, wears a pattern that mimics lichen, and rests, camouflaged, on the very surface that fed it as a caterpillar. Few insects tie their diet, their disguise and their habitat together quite so neatly, and fewer still reward the kind of close, unhurried looking that a graphite drawing demands of both artist and viewer.

Sent as a note or kept as a small study in tone rather than colour, the card suits anyone who prefers their natural history a little overlooked and a little quiet, rather than bright or showy.
