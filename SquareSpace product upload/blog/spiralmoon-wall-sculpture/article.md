# A circle that never quite closes

Spiralmoon takes as its subject something that sounds like a contradiction: a shape that is smooth and circular, and yet, almost, spiralling. A single panel, 70cm by 50cm, carved and cast in white marble stone, the piece renders the Moon as a calm, rounded form with just enough of a turn worked into it to suggest continuous, ongoing motion rather than a fixed disc.

There is a genuine astronomical idea behind that impression. The Moon's orbit around Earth looks, from Earth, like a simple closed circle, repeating every lunar month. But the Moon is not only orbiting Earth. Earth, and the Moon along with it, is also orbiting the Sun, at a considerably faster pace than the Moon circles Earth. Trace the Moon's actual path through space relative to the Sun, rather than relative to Earth, and the picture changes: because the Sun's gravity on the Moon is, in fact, stronger than Earth's, the Moon's real trajectory through space never loops back on itself the way a simple circle around Earth would suggest. Instead, it follows Earth's own path around the Sun in a long, gently scalloped curve, always curving toward the Sun, dipping slightly inward and outward as it weaves back and forth across Earth's orbital path once a month, but never once doubling back on itself. Seen this way, the Moon's true journey is closer to a very slow, very wide, almost spiralling loop around the Sun than to tight little circles around the Earth, a genuinely different picture of lunar motion from the one most people carry in their heads.

Carved by hand and then cast in white marble stone, Spiralmoon builds that sense of continuous turning into a single, generous curve across the panel, avoiding any hard stop or seam that might read as a completed, closed shape. At 7cm deep, the relief holds enough shadow along the curve to keep it reading as three-dimensional and in motion rather than as a flat printed circle, particularly as the light moves across it through the day. Morning and evening light draws the spiralling line out in sharp relief; the flatter light of midday lets the same curve soften into a calmer, more restful shape, closer to the serenity the sculpture is named for.

At 70cm by 50cm, Spiralmoon works well as a single anchor piece on a wall without a natural partner sculpture — a living room, a bedroom, or a study wall where a calm, continuous form is wanted rather than a busier composition. It suits both interior placement and a garden wall, and, like the rest of the collection, rewards a spot that receives some changing light through the day rather than one lit flatly and evenly at all times.
