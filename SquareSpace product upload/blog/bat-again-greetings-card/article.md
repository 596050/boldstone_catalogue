# Out Again at Dusk

Every bat in Britain does the same thing every single evening it is active: it wakes, waits for the light to drop low enough, and leaves the roost it has spent the day hanging in, upside down, to go hunting again. "Again" is doing real work in that sentence, because it is exactly right — not a one-off flight but a nightly repetition, driven tightly enough by the fading light that bat surveys are timed to the minute around sunset, watching for that first shape peeling away from a roost entrance as dusk sets in.

What makes the shape worth watching is unlike anything else sharing the sky with it. A bat's wing is not a feathered structure built new for flight the way a bird's is; it is a hand, dramatically stretched, with four elongated fingers spread wide and a thin double membrane of skin drawn taut between them and down to the ankle. Where a bird flies with a wing built from a forearm and fused finger bones, a bat flies, quite literally, by spreading its fingers and gliding on the skin between them — the only mammal that has ever managed powered flight rather than the more limited gliding some other mammals, like flying squirrels, get by with instead.

Most bats hunting at dusk are relying on a sense that has no real equivalent in human experience: echolocation, a stream of calls pitched above the range of human hearing, bounced off insects and obstacles and read on the way back with enough precision to catch a moving moth in complete darkness. The trade-off most people never think about is that a bat's eyes still work perfectly well — the old saying that bats are blind is simply false — but sound has proven the more reliable tool for hunting fast, small, moving targets at night, so bats have leaned into it rather than relying on sight alone.

Folklore has never quite settled on how to feel about any of this. Bats have been read as omens, as symbols of the underworld, as the animal familiar of choice in stories about witches, and, in some traditions, as symbols of good fortune and longevity instead — an unusually wide spread of meaning for one small nocturnal mammal, probably earned by the simple fact that something flying competently through total darkness, doing something no other local mammal can manage, was always going to attract more than its fair share of stories.

This piece, credited to TP of TONK, takes the bat back to its most ordinary, least symbolic moment: not a supernatural apparition, simply an animal going back out to work at the time of day it always goes back out to work, again, as it does every clear evening of its active season.

Sent as a note, it suits anyone who likes a subject with a bit of nerve to it rather than a conventionally pretty one.
