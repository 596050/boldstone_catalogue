# The shape a hillside takes under cultivation

Cultivation Folds reads a set of curving, folded lines not as drapery or a fan, but as land: the stepped, level bands cut into a steep hillside wherever farmers have terraced a slope to grow crops on ground that would otherwise be too steep to hold water or soil. Carved as a single panel, 70cm by 50cm, in white marble stone, the piece renders that landscape logic in relief, each fold reading as one more contour cut into the hill.

Terracing is one of the oldest and most effective pieces of agricultural engineering available to a farmer without machinery. Cut a series of level steps into a hillside, build a low retaining edge along the front of each one, and a slope that would otherwise shed rainwater and topsoil straight downhill instead holds both in place, step by step, long enough for a crop to grow. The technique is old and widespread, developed independently by farming communities working steep, mountainous ground across the world, from the flooded rice terraces cut into hillsides in the Philippines and Bali to the stepped paddies of southern China's uplands. Seen from a distance, or from above, the result is a landscape that reads as a series of curving bands following the natural contour of the land — which is, not coincidentally, exactly the same principle a map uses when it draws contour lines to show elevation: a line joining every point at the same height. A terraced hillside is, in effect, a contour map made real, built by hand rather than drawn.

Cultivation Folds is carved by hand and then cast in white marble stone, its curved bands kept soft-edged rather than sharply geometric, closer to the eroded, hand-built irregularity of a real terraced hillside than to a drafted architectural line. That softness affects how the piece takes light: rather than throwing one crisp shadow line per fold, the curves shade gradually from light to dark, so the whole relief reads more like a landscape seen from a distance than a pattern seen up close. As the light moves through the day, the folds deepen and flatten in turn, echoing the way a real terraced hillside changes character between the flat light of midday and the raking gold of early morning or evening, when every terrace edge throws its own long shadow across the one below it.

At 70cm by 50cm, Cultivation Folds works as a single anchor piece, well suited to a kitchen wall, a dining room, or a garden wall, where its landscape-like quality can sit comfortably near planting or other natural-form pieces without competing with anything sharply geometric nearby. Like the rest of the collection, it benefits from some changing natural light through the day rather than a single static downlight.
