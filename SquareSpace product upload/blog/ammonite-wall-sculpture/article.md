# The shell that doubled as a submarine

Ammonite renders the same basic subject as its larger companion piece elsewhere in this collection, but at a smaller, more domestic scale: two panels, each 52cm by 25cm, carved and cast in white marble stone, together spanning 50cm in height, the coiled shell of an extinct sea creature held in quiet relief.

An ammonite's coiled shell was not simply a shield. It was, in effect, a piece of biological engineering for controlling depth. The shell was divided into a series of internal chambers, and the animal itself lived only in the outermost, largest one, the older, smaller chambers behind it left empty apart from a mix of gas and liquid whose proportions the animal could adjust through a thin strand of living tissue called the siphuncle, which ran back through all the chambers via small holes in each dividing wall. By altering the ratio of gas to liquid in those chambers, an ammonite could fine-tune its buoyancy and rise or sink through the water column with comparatively little muscular effort, in much the same functional principle, arrived at hundreds of millions of years earlier, that a submarine now uses its ballast tanks to control depth. It is a detail easy to miss looking at a fossil from the outside, where only the coiled, ridged shell is visible, but it means the shape carved into this sculpture once housed a genuinely sophisticated piece of natural machinery, not just a passive coil of shell.

Ammonites varied enormously in size across their long history. Most species found today are modest, a few centimetres to perhaps a hand's span across, but the group also produced some genuine giants: fossils of Parapuzosia seppenradensis, found in Germany, reach shell diameters approaching two metres, making them among the largest shelled animals of any kind known to have existed. The two-part sculpture here keeps to a domestic, wearable scale rather than the giants of the fossil record, but it is worth knowing the same basic coiled design, chambered shell, buoyancy-controlling siphuncle, logarithmic spiral growth, was shared by species ranging from the size of a coin to the size of a cartwheel.

The two panels are carved by hand and then cast in white marble stone, each holding part of the coiled form so the two together complete the spiral, much as a real ammonite fossil, split or weathered along its coiling plane, is often found and sold in the fossil trade as a pair of matching halves, one showing the exterior ridges, the other hinting at the chambers within. Through the day, the ridged growth lines of the shell catch a low, raking light most clearly, each ridge throwing its own fine shadow, while flatter light softens the whole coil into a more continuous curve.

At 52cm by 50cm across the pair, Ammonite is a comfortably domestic size, suited to a hallway, a study, or a smaller living room wall, and works equally well indoors or on a sheltered outdoor wall.
