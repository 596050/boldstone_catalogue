# The Maple Before the Fire

Ask anyone to picture a Japanese maple and they will most likely picture red — the fierce, saturated scarlet the tree is famous for putting on in autumn, the reason gardens and temple grounds built entire visiting seasons around it. This photograph is interested in a different, quieter register: the cooler blue-green cast the same leaves carry earlier in the year, before that autumn transformation begins. It is a portrait of the tree at rest, doing its ordinary summer work, rather than at its single most photographed moment.

Acer palmatum, the Japanese maple, is defined visually by its palmate leaf — divided into five, seven, sometimes nine narrow, pointed lobes radiating from a central point, like a spread hand, a shape that has made it one of the most instantly recognisable tree leaves anywhere in temperate gardening. What is less often appreciated is how much that leaf's colour shifts across a single season, quite apart from the famous autumn display: many cultivars open with a reddish flush in spring, settle into a cooler green or blue-green through summer as chlorophyll production takes over, and only begin breaking that green down again as autumn approaches, unmasking the red and gold pigments that were present all along but simply outnumbered by chlorophyll until the days shorten and the tree stops replenishing it. The blue-toned leaf this photograph shows is the tree mid-story, not at its ending.

Japan's own tradition of maple viewing, momijigari, treats this tree with a seriousness matched by relatively few other single species anywhere in gardening culture, running as a direct seasonal counterpart to the spring cherry blossom viewing, hanami, that is more widely known outside Japan. Where hanami is built around a famously brief bloom, momijigari follows a slower unfolding, and centuries of selective breeding in Japan have produced an extraordinary range of Acer palmatum cultivars, differing in leaf shape, from broad and simple to finely dissected into lace-like filaments, and in colour through every stage of the growing year, not only the autumn finish most outside admirers wait for.

This is, in part, why a study of the tree's summer colour is worth making at all. The blue-green phase gets far less attention than the autumn fire, precisely because it is not the moment anyone travels to see, yet it is the colour the tree actually wears for most of the months it is in leaf — arguably a more honest portrait of what living with a Japanese maple day to day actually looks like than the brief, spectacular week or two of red that dominates its reputation.

Photographing that cooler register asks for a different kind of light than the low, warm, backlit sun that makes autumn foliage glow. A more even, slightly overcast light suits blue-green foliage better, letting the colour read as genuinely cool rather than being warmed artificially by golden-hour sun — a quieter photographic decision to match the quieter colour being recorded.
