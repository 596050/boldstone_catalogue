# A Colour That Is Never Actually Mixed

Green is an easy colour to make with a brush: blue and yellow paint stirred together on a palette until the two become one new, uniform hue. Marbling will not allow that shortcut. Pigments floated on a size bath sit on the surface rather than dissolving into it, which means two different coloured drops placed near one another never truly blend into a third colour the way wet paint on a palette does. What looks like green in a finished marbled piece is very often blue and yellow sitting immediately beside each other in fine, interlocking rings or veins, close enough that the eye does the mixing rather than the pigment, a genuine case of optical blending rather than physical mixing, not unlike the way a length of woven cloth can appear to be a single colour from across a room while being made of two different coloured threads up close.

Green moons, meanwhile, are not really a natural phenomenon so much as a recurring illusion, and a useful one for understanding how easily the eye can be fooled by context. The moon's actual surface colour, under neutral lighting, is a fairly uniform grey, sometimes with a faint brownish cast from iron-rich minerals in its soil. Reports of a moon looking distinctly green are almost always explained by something happening in Earth's own atmosphere between the observer and the moon, rather than by the moon itself: haze, smoke, or dust of a particular particle size can shift the colour of transmitted moonlight in unpredictable ways, and the human eye is also simply unreliable at judging the true colour of a dim light source seen in isolation against a dark sky, prone to shifting its perceived colour depending on what else is nearby for comparison. It is a helpful reminder that a great deal of what people report seeing in the sky, in colour terms, says as much about atmosphere and perception as it does about the object itself.
