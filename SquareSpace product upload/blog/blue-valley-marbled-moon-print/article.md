# A Crack Wide Enough to Be Named

Where a comb pulled through a marbling bath produces a regular, repeating wave, a valley pattern in marbling usually comes from a broader, slower tool, a wide rake or the flat of a stylus, drawn once through the floating colour to open a distinct channel between two ridges of pigment, deeper and less regular than a fine comb pattern. The effect depends on the bath being left otherwise undisturbed around it, so the eye reads the channel as a genuine dip in a continuous surface, even though the whole pattern is, in reality, no more than a thin skin of pigment floating flat on the size below it.

The moon has its own named valleys, and the grandest of them was cut not by water or by the wave-like lava flows that formed the rilles nearby, but by a more sudden, violent process: faulting. Vallis Alpes, the Alpine Valley, cuts a long, straight-sided gash across the lunar highlands north of the large crater Plato, wide enough and geometrically regular enough that early telescopic observers argued for decades over whether it could really be natural. The current understanding is that it is a graben, a block of crust that dropped down between two roughly parallel faults, most likely as the enormous impact basin now filled by the Mare Imbrium formed nearby and fractured the surrounding crust under the sheer scale of that impact. A much narrower rille, cut later by lava, runs down the floor of the valley itself, so Vallis Alpes is really two different processes, tectonic fracturing and volcanic flow, recorded one inside the other in the same feature.
