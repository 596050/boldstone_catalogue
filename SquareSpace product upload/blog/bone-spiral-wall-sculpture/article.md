# The spiral that is already inside you

Bone Spiral works from a single idea: that many of nature's spirals are not just surface decoration but structural, built into the skeleton and architecture of living things rather than applied afterwards. A two-part wall sculpture, its larger panel 55cm by 55cm and its smaller 55cm by 30cm, carved and cast in white marble stone, spans 85cm together, the spiral held within a solid, bone-like mass rather than floating free.

Spirals turn up inside bodies more often than most people realise. The cochlea, the part of the inner ear responsible for translating sound into nerve signals, is a small coiled tube shaped unmistakably like a tiny snail shell, its spiral form allowing a long, sound-sensitive membrane to be packed into a very small space within the skull. Certain horned mammals grow their horns as a continuous helical spiral rather than a straight spike — a kudu's horns twist through more than two full turns as they grow, and a ram's horns curl in a tightening spiral for the animal's whole life, made of the same keratin as hair and fingernail rather than true bone, but built by the same principle of steady, asymmetric growth that produces a shell's spiral. And beneath all of it, at the molecular scale, the DNA coiled inside every one of those cells is itself a double helix, two strands wound around a shared axis, arguably the most fundamental spiral of all, the one that carries the instructions for every bone, horn and shell mentioned above. Bone Spiral takes its name from that idea: the spiral is not just something nature occasionally decorates its surfaces with, but a shape woven into structure itself, from the scale of a horn down to the scale of a molecule.

The two panels are carved individually and then cast in white marble stone, deliberately kept at different sizes rather than as a matched pair, echoing the way a real skeletal spiral, like a horn or a cochlea, grows unevenly rather than symmetrically. At 7cm deep, there is enough thickness to let the spiral genuinely recede into the stone, so its centre sits in real shadow rather than merely being suggested by an engraved line. Through the day, that depth means the piece holds its shadow even under fairly flat light, growing more dramatic as the sun lowers and rakes across the curved form.

With its two panels of different sizes, Bone Spiral asks for a slightly more considered hang than a simple matched pair. The larger and smaller panels need to sit in a proportionate relationship to each other, with a gap that lets each read as a distinct mass rather than an awkward mismatch. At 55cm by 85cm together, it suits a study, a hallway, or a wall where a single, weighty sculptural idea is wanted rather than a wide horizontal band.
