# A leaf, and the shadow it was already casting

Two Leaf Forms takes as its subject not one thing but two: a leaf, and the shadow that leaf throws once light falls across it. Two panels, each 35cm square, carved and cast in white marble stone, together spanning 70cm, hold that doubled idea quite literally — leaf shape and leaf shadow, rendered in the one medium already built entirely from light and shadow.

A leaf's outline is rarely arbitrary. Running through it is a branching network of veins, usually one central midrib with lateral veins forking off at intervals and dividing again into ever finer branches, that does two jobs at once: distributing water and sugars through the leaf, and giving the thin blade enough structural rigidity to hold its shape against wind and its own weight rather than collapsing. Different plant families have settled on quite different solutions to this problem. Broad-leaved trees typically show a netted, reticulate pattern of veins branching and rejoining across the whole blade, while grasses and other monocots run their veins in parallel lines from base to tip. Botanists still use leaf shape and venation pattern as one of the more reliable ways to identify a plant even without its flowers, because the outline and internal structure of a leaf tend to be as distinctive as a fingerprint within a species. The shadow a leaf casts is a separate, equally real phenomenon: light falling through a gap in foliage creates the dappled patches of sun and shade known to naturalists as sunflecks, a shifting mosaic on the ground that changes constantly as the sun moves and the leaves themselves stir in the wind.

Carved by hand and then cast, the two panels in white marble stone hold both leaf and shadow as raised and recessed form rather than colour, which is really the point of the piece: a leaf shadow in nature has no fixed shape of its own, it is simply the leaf's outline projected onto whatever it falls across, distorted by the angle of the light. Here, that logic is folded permanently into the stone itself, so the sculpture's own cast shadow, changing through the day as real light moves across it, becomes a second, ever-changing shadow layered on top of the carved one. Morning and evening light picks out the edges of both leaf and shadow sharply; the flatter light of midday softens the distinction between the two, letting them merge into a single quiet texture.

At 35cm x 70cm across the pair, Two Leaf Forms is a modestly scaled piece well suited to a hallway, a smaller wall, or grouped alongside other similarly sized reliefs. Because much of its interest lies in the interplay between carved shadow and cast shadow, it benefits from a wall that receives some natural, moving daylight rather than one lit only by a single fixed spotlight.
