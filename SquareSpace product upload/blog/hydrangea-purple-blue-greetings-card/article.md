# The Flower That Reads the Soil

A hydrangea head rarely settles for one colour, and this photograph does not pretend otherwise. Sally Price's image holds a single flower head at the point where purple shades into blue, floret by floret, rather than choosing a moment when the colour has resolved into something simpler. Look closely and the transition is not smooth gradient but a mosaic — dozens of small four-petalled florets, each one landing at a slightly different point on the same colour scale, the whole head reading as a single colour only from a distance.

That instability is not a fault of the photograph. It is the plant's actual chemistry on display. Most garden hydrangeas of the mophead and lacecap type — Hydrangea macrophylla and its relatives — carry a pigment that is naturally pink, but the flower's true colour depends on whether aluminium ions are available to it in the soil and able to bind to that pigment. In acidic soil, aluminium becomes soluble and the plant can take it up through its roots, and the pigment shifts toward blue and violet. In alkaline soil, aluminium stays locked up and unavailable, and the same pigment reads pink. Somewhere in between, on soil that is only moderately acidic, a plant will produce both effects in different parts of the same head, which is precisely the purple-into-blue gradient caught here — not two flowers, but one flower negotiating with its own soil in real time. It is one of the few common garden plants that functions, without any instrument, as a rough pH indicator for the ground it grows in.

The structure underneath that colour is worth a second look too. What reads from a distance as a single "flower" is in fact a dense head of many small flowers packed together, and in the lacecap varieties a hydrangea head is more clearly divided into two kinds of floret: a flat, understated centre of small fertile flowers doing the actual work of producing seed, ringed by a showier outer band of large, sterile florets whose only job is to be conspicuous enough to draw in pollinating insects. The mophead varieties, more familiar in British gardens, take that showy outer ring and let it fill the whole head, sterile florets crowding out the plainer fertile centre almost entirely — an entire flower head built, essentially, from advertising.

Photographing that structure honestly means resisting the urge to flatten it into a single wash of colour. The image keeps the gradation between florets legible, so the eye can move across the head and register the shift in tone rather than absorbing it as one even colour — closer to how the flower actually reads in daylight, when a hydrangea head rewards close attention rather than a glance from across the border. Photography, unlike a hand-mixed wash, records the specific, unrepeatable colour a specific plant produced on a specific day in its own particular soil — this hydrangea, not hydrangeas in general.

Native originally to East Asia, hydrangeas have become one of the fixtures of the British and European garden, particularly in the softer, damper conditions of coastal and western gardens where the plant is most at home, and the colour-by-soil trick has made them a long-standing favourite for gardeners who enjoy having some, if not total, say over what their flowers will do.
