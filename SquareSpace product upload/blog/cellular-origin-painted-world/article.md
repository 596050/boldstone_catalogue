# The Pattern Under the Skin

Look closely enough at almost any thin, papery plant surface and it stops looking smooth. It resolves into a grid: small compartments, each with a visible wall, packed edge to edge like a honeycomb or a badly laid patio. That grid is one of the oldest and most reproducible sights in natural history, and it is the pattern this 40cm watercolour and ink study, worked on 100% cotton rag paper and limited to an edition of one, sets out to look at directly.

Plant cells were first named from exactly this kind of surface. In the 1660s, examining thin slices of cork bark under an early microscope, the pattern of small regular compartments reminded its observer of the tiny rooms in a monastery, and the word "cell" entered biology from that comparison. The demonstration that generations of school pupils have repeated since is not cork but onion: peel the thin, transparent skin from between the layers of an onion bulb, lay it flat on a slide, and under even a modest microscope the cell walls resolve into that same tessellated grid, with the oval shadow of a nucleus visible inside many of the compartments. It works as a first demonstration precisely because the tissue is only one cell thick and almost transparent, so light passes straight through the walls rather than being blocked by the many layers a thicker leaf or stem would present. Every plant, from the highest tree to the humblest bulb, is built from repeating units of exactly this kind, each one a small enclosed compartment doing its own chemistry and dividing to make more of itself. It is a genuinely foundational image in the history of biology, not a decorative one, and painting it at a large, legible scale gives it a presence a microscope slide never has.

Rendering cell structure in watercolour and ink plays to what both media do well. Ink can hold the thin, continuous line of a cell wall without it thickening or breaking, the way a wash alone tends to. Watercolour then fills each compartment with its own slightly different value or hue, close enough to its neighbours to read as one continuous tissue but varied enough that the pattern does not flatten into a mechanical grid. Cotton rag paper takes both processes without the paper's own texture fighting the fine linework, which matters more here than in a looser, broader wash.
