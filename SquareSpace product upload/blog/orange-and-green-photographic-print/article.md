# The Colour Argument of a Single Bloom

Photograph a flower against foliage and you are photographing an argument that colour has been having with itself for millions of years. Orange and green sit close together on the colour wheel, near neighbours rather than opposites, which is exactly why a single orange bloom held above a mass of green stem and leaf reads as vividly as it does. The eye has to work slightly harder to separate a warm hue from a colour that shares some of its underlying wavelength, and that extra work is what makes the image hold your attention rather than let it slide past.

This print is a close study of exactly that relationship: an exotic flower, its petals a clean, saturated orange, set against the cooler green that surrounds it. Tropical and subtropical flowers of this kind tend to favour bold, uncomplicated colour and strong structural form over the layered pastel subtlety of a temperate garden bloom, waxy petal surfaces that hold a highlight rather than absorb it, and a simplified outline that reads clearly even at a distance. Orange pigmentation in flowers generally comes from carotenoids, the same family of compounds responsible for the colour of a carrot or an autumn leaf once its chlorophyll has broken down, a genuinely ancient pigment, chemically stable, and part of why orange flowers tend to hold their colour so well in strong sunlight rather than bleaching out.

Getting a photograph like this right is mostly a question of depth of field. Focus falls on the bloom itself, the fall of light across the curve of a petal, the slight translucency at its thinnest edge where light passes through rather than bouncing off, while the green behind it is allowed to soften into an unfocused field of colour rather than a competing set of shapes. That softness is doing real work: it is what separates a considered photograph from a snapshot, and it is only achievable with a lens working close to its subject at a wide aperture, which is also why the detail on the flower itself is sharp enough to show texture in the petal that you would otherwise walk straight past.

The print itself is produced on aluminium dibond, a rigid aluminium composite panel rather than paper, finished matt rather than gloss. Dibond takes a photographic image with genuine flatness; there is no cockling or bowing over time the way paper mounted under glass can develop, and the matt finish means the piece can hang opposite a window without throwing back a wash of reflected light the way a gloss print or a glazed frame would. It also means no glass and a surface that can be wiped clean, a real practical advantage in a kitchen, which is exactly where this piece is suited to sit well, though it holds its own equally in a hallway or a home office wall that gets strong, indirect daylight.

At 60cm x 40cm, it has enough presence to work as a single statement piece on an otherwise plain wall, without needing a partner. Because there is no glazing and no frame required, it hangs flush to the wall on a simple fixing, which keeps the presentation modern and slightly architectural rather than domestic, in keeping with the contemporary, clean-lined rooms this kind of print tends to suit best. Strong colour like this rewards a fairly neutral wall behind it: white, grey, or a pale warm plaster tone will let the orange do the work rather than fighting it for attention.
