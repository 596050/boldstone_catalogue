# Speed, held perfectly still

Two Frozen Decowaves borrows a trick from the industrial design of the 1930s: the suggestion of speed and movement built into an object that never actually moves at all. Two long, narrow panels, each 90cm by 15cm, carve a wave shape into white marble stone with the hard, confident geometry of the Art Deco period rather than the soft irregularity of an actual sea, the pair spanning 30cm in height once hung with a gap between them.

The 1930s produced a design vocabulary sometimes called Streamline Moderne, in which even objects that had no need whatsoever to be aerodynamic — pencil sharpeners, radio cabinets, kitchen toasters, building façades — were given the swept-back curves and parallel speed lines of a fast train or aircraft. The style borrowed its logic from genuinely streamlined subjects of the era: express locomotives, aeroplanes, and the great transatlantic ocean liners of the decade, ships whose interiors were fitted out in exactly this bold, geometric Deco style, with wave and speed motifs worked into everything from stair rails to light fittings. A frozen wave rendered as a set of parallel, hard-edged curves belongs to that same family of idea: motion suggested through repetition and clean line, rather than through the actual unevenness of moving water. It is a wave with the randomness edited out, redrawn with the confidence of an engineer's drawing rather than a photograph.

The two panels are carved by hand, then cast, in white marble stone, kept to a firm 8cm depth that allows a genuinely crisp edge along each wave form. That depth matters for the geometry to read correctly: a shallower cut would soften the deliberately hard lines into something closer to a natural ripple, undoing the effect the style depends on. Through the day, the raking light of morning or evening throws a long, sharp-edged shadow along each wave, while overhead light flattens the piece into a more even, graphic pattern, closer to how the same motif would have read printed flat on a poster or worked into a piece of period furniture.

At 90cm wide per panel, the two parts read as a long, low, horizontal band well suited to a kitchen wall, above a run of low cabinetry, or a narrow strip of wall where a taller sculpture would not fit. Hung with a consistent gap, the pair totals 30cm in height, making it one of the more elongated, restrained shapes in the collection — a piece that works as a quiet horizontal line across a room rather than a dominant focal point.
