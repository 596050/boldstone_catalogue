# Why Fish, Moons and Waves Keep Returning

Fish do not swim alone if they can help it. A shoal moving as one is harder for a predator to track, harder to single out one target from, and more efficient through the water than the same number of fish moving individually, a piece of collective behaviour so effective that it shows up, in one form or another, across most of the ocean's open-water species. It is also, visually, one of the more mesmerising things water does: hundreds of small bodies turning together in a single fluid gesture, catching and losing the light in waves of their own that have nothing to do with the sea's actual waves moving above them. This sculpture takes that image, fish moving beneath the surface, under moonlight rather than sun, and renders it in relief.

The moon's relationship with the sea runs deeper than reflected light on water, though that alone would be reason enough for the two to appear together here. Tidal rhythm is lunar, and a number of marine species time their spawning to specific phases of the moon, aligning huge, synchronised reproductive events to a lunar calendar that has nothing to do with daylight. None of that needs to be known to read the piece, but it is the quiet logic underneath a subject that pairs a shoal of fish with a moon overhead: two things governed by the same tide, the same cycle, moving to a rhythm neither of them controls.

Moons, spirals, waves and shells recur constantly across this kind of work, and it is worth asking why. Partly it is because these are shapes the eye is already fluent in, a spiral shoal, a shell's whorl, a moon's curve, are all close relatives, variations on a spiral that shows up again and again wherever growth happens at a steady rate around a fixed point. Relief carving is particularly suited to this kind of form, because a spiral or a curve reads entirely through the shadow it casts rather than through any colour or surface pattern, light raking across a carved curve does the describing, the way moonlight itself would.

This piece is carved by hand and then cast, in a white marble stone finish that takes light in exactly that way: raking, low-angled light, early or late in the day, or from a single lamp at night, throws long shadows across the carved recesses and picks out the raised curve of the fish and moon forms sharply, while flat, overhead daylight flattens the same piece into a much quieter, softer read. That is the basic fact of relief as a medium: it has no fixed appearance. It changes through the day as the light moves across it, and it changes again from season to season as the sun's angle shifts, which is a different kind of object to live with than a flat photograph or painting that looks the same at every hour.
