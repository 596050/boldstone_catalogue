# A Surface That Changes With the Room

Most watercolours hold still. This one does not, not entirely, because gold leaf is not a pigment and does not behave like one. Gold Pond Painted World combines watercolour and ink with applied gold leaf on 100% cotton rag paper, a single 40cm square set inside a round window cut through its mount board, and the gold is the reason the piece will never look quite the same twice. Pigment absorbs and reflects light in a fixed way regardless of where you stand. Metal leaf reflects it directionally, so the painting shifts in brightness and warmth as the light in the room changes through the day, or as you walk past it.

The title points to water, and still water is one of the few natural surfaces that behaves the way gold leaf does: not as a colour in itself but as a mirror for whatever light falls on it. A pond at low sun can turn the colour of hammered metal, not because the water has changed but because its surface is now catching light at a low, glancing angle and throwing most of it straight back rather than letting it scatter and penetrate. Water at that angle stops being transparent and becomes reflective, the same shift a lake makes between a bright noon, when you can see straight down into it, and an evening, when the surface turns to a sheet of colour and the depths disappear. Gilding a painted surface borrows that same optical trick deliberately: an illuminator working on a mediaeval manuscript, or Klimt building up a portrait background decades later, both used gold leaf for exactly this reason, to put a passage into the painting that behaves like a light source rather than a fixed patch of colour.

Applying gold leaf to a watercolour ground is a controlled, patient process. The leaf itself is beaten to a few hundred atoms thick, thin enough to tear at a breath, and it is laid onto a prepared, tacky size rather than painted or brushed like pigment, then burnished once it has taken. Cotton rag paper gives the size something stable and slightly toothed to grip, which is part of why this series is built on it rather than a lighter or more mechanically finished sheet: the paper has to survive both a watercolour wash and a gilding pass without buckling or losing its surface.

Because the gold is reflective rather than fixed, where you hang this piece changes what it does more than it would for an ordinary print. Even, moderate daylight, rather than a single hard spotlight, lets the gold shift gently through the day instead of glaring in one direction and going dead in every other. A hallway or landing that gets soft light from a side window suits it better than a wall lit by a single downlight aimed straight at the glass, which will bounce hard off the gold and gilt everything else out. Glazing should still be UV-filtering to protect the watercolour and ink beneath the gold, and it is worth choosing glass with lower reflectivity where possible, so the room's own light does not compete with the gold leaf for the eye's attention.

The mount is a round window cut into high quality board, offered in a choice of colours, with an acid-free card backing board keeping the paper stable behind the glass. No frame is included, which suits a piece like this, since the right frame for a gilded surface depends entirely on what else gold or metal is doing in the room.
