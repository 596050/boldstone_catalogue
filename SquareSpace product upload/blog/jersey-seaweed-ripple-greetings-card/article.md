# The Weed the Islands Used to Live On

Seaweed washed up on a Jersey beach used to be worth collecting, and not for the table. For centuries, islanders held the right to cut and gather seaweed from the shore under a system known locally as vraicing — vraic being the Jèrriais and Norman-French word for the seaweed itself — dried or burned to ash and dug straight into the fields as fertiliser. Collection did not simply begin whenever anyone fancied it; a marker known as lé mèr had to be uncovered by the tide before cutting could start, and the harvest was divided into weed cut fresh from the rocks and drift-weed gathered after a storm, each valued slightly differently depending on the time of year. Jersey's well-known Royal potatoes are still, by some accounts, partly indebted to that old practice, the seaweed's minerals said to contribute to a flavour distinctive enough to have earned the crop protected status.

None of that history needs to be known to enjoy a photograph of rippled sand and stranded weed on a Jersey beach, but it changes what the image is quietly documenting: not simply a pretty coastal texture, but the raw material of an entire agricultural tradition, one that shaped the rhythm of a working year on the island for far longer than tourism has existed as an industry there. Vraicing has never entirely stopped, continuing today on a much smaller scale, but the sight of a beach thick with weed after a big tide once meant something closer to a harvest than a view.

The ripple itself, meanwhile, is doing its own quiet piece of natural engineering. Water moving rhythmically across loose sand — whether pushed by waves or simply by the retreating tide — sorts grains by size and weight into evenly spaced ridges, the same physical process, running at a different scale, that produces ripples in windblown desert sand and the crossbedded patterns visible in some sedimentary rock. A rippled beach is essentially a snapshot of moving water, made momentarily solid enough to photograph, right up until the next tide rearranges it entirely.

Together, weed and ripple make a photograph about a shoreline doing two things at once: recording the passage of water in the sand, and holding, in the weed itself, the raw material of one of the Channel Islands' oldest working traditions. Neither element needs the other explained to be worth looking at, but knowing both makes the image considerably richer than a simple beach scene.
