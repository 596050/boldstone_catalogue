# The Half of the Plant Nobody Draws

Every other card in this series gives the eye something to admire above the bulb: a green shoot climbing for the light, a flower bud swelling toward bloom. This one turns the whole composition the other way and looks down, at the pale, wiry tangle of roots most drawings of a sprouting onion leave out entirely, because by the time an onion is usually drawn it has been lifted, trimmed, and cleaned of exactly this kind of untidy detail.

Those roots are not decorative loose ends. An onion left to sprout in storage, especially one sitting in a little water or damp compost rather than dry air, will often push out fresh roots from the flattened base plate at the same time it pushes a shoot upward — two separate growth events from two ends of the same small patch of tissue, driven by different instructions. The shoot grows up because it is following light and working against gravity. The roots grow down and outward because they are following an entirely separate set of cues: gravity again, but pulling the other way, and moisture, sensed and chased through a mechanism called hydrotropism, in which a root can detect a gradient of water in the surrounding medium and bend its growth toward the wetter side. A bulb sitting in a saucer with only its base touching water will send roots specifically toward that dampness, adjusting course as they grow, in a slow, blind, wholly effective negotiation with wherever water happens to be.

The roots themselves are doing more structural work than their thin, unglamorous appearance suggests. Look closely — closer than most kitchen encounters with an onion ever require — and each root is covered along its length with a fine fuzz of root hairs: microscopic extensions of single cells, vastly increasing the surface area in contact with water and soil without costing the plant much material to grow. It is root hairs, not the visible root itself, that do most of the actual absorbing, and they are so delicate that they are usually torn away and lost the moment a plant is pulled from the ground — which is one reason gardeners are told to ease seedlings out gently rather than yank them, and one reason this fine, hair-fringed structure so rarely survives long enough to be sketched from life.

There is something quietly corrective about a drawing that includes all this. Most botanical attention, in art as much as in a vegetable rack, goes to whatever is reaching upward and visible: the shoot, the leaf, the eventual flower. Roots get noticed mainly when something goes wrong above ground and a gardener finally digs down to see why. Giving the root system the same careful, unhurried attention as the shoot corrects that imbalance, if only for the length of one drawing — a reminder that the visible, photogenic half of a plant is doing exactly half the work, and frequently the easier half.

Watercolour suits this pale, tangled subject well: thin, dry-brush lines for the individual root threads, a little wet-in-wet softness where they cluster and cross, restraint rather than heavy colour, since roots have almost none to offer and do not need any invented for them.
