# Making a Case for the Onion

No one grows onions for their looks. They are a store cupboard staple valued for keeping through winter, for the sharp bite they bring to a dish, for being cheap and reliable rather than for any quality anyone would call beautiful. Which is exactly the tradition this card belongs to: still life painting, particularly the Dutch and Flemish work of the seventeenth century, built entire careers out of proving that ordinary kitchen produce — onions, herring, bread, a chipped pewter jug — could sustain exactly the same quality of looking usually reserved for grander subjects, if the artist paid close enough attention to the object actually in front of them rather than to what convention said was worth painting.

A sprouting onion argues its own case, if given the chance. The bulb's tightly wrapped layers, visible where the papery outer skin has begun to lift and fall away, catch light in bands of amber and cream that shift subtly with each layer's slight difference in density and moisture. The green shoot breaking from the crown adds a colour and a texture nothing else in the composition offers — smooth, saturated, upright against the softer, rounder bulk of the bulb beneath it. None of this needed the onion to be rare, expensive or exotic. It only needed someone willing to look at it for longer than the time it takes to peel one for supper.

Watercolour and drawing combined, as this piece does, gives the subject two different kinds of truth at once. Drawing can hold the crisp structural facts — where one layer ends and the next begins, the exact curve of the shoot — while watercolour supplies the softer, more atmospheric qualities: the slight bloom of colour across a papery skin, the way light seems to sit inside the bulb rather than merely on its surface. Together they do more justice to a genuinely humble vegetable than either medium would manage alone.

There is a reasonable argument that kitchen vegetables make better subjects than flowers precisely because nobody expects them to be beautiful going in. A rose arrives with expectations already attached; an onion arrives with none, so any beauty a viewer eventually finds in it feels earned rather than assumed, a small private discovery rather than a foregone conclusion. It is a lower bar to clear, and clearing it anyway counts for more.

This card belongs to a small set following one onion through an unplanned second life in storage, and this instalment makes the broadest claim of the four: that the whole subject, examined properly, was worth the attention regardless of which particular stage it is caught at.
