# The Channels the Moon's Lava Left Behind

The regular, undulating wave pattern running through this piece is made with a comb, drawn once through floating rings of colour on the marbling bath in a single continuous pull. Where a stripe pattern uses a comb with evenly spaced, straight teeth pulled in one direction, a wave or "nonpareil" pattern is built by combing the colour first one way and then, in a second pass at a different angle or with a different tool, drawing it into the undulating, ribbon-like form the technique is named for. Both passes have to happen while the paint is still wet and floating; the entire pattern is fixed within seconds of the final comb stroke, before the sheet of paper is lowered onto the surface to lift the whole design off in one piece.

Waves of a different kind cross the lunar surface too, not on it but through the solid rock, in the form of sinuous rilles: long, winding, canyon-like channels that snake across the dark lava plains, the maria, in patterns that look startlingly like a dry riverbed. They are not river channels, since the moon has never had flowing water, but the channels cut by something almost as fluid: lava. Early in its history the moon experienced enormous basaltic eruptions, floods of low-viscosity lava that behaved much like water, flowing downhill and cutting or roofing over channels as they went, some of which later collapsed into open troughs once the lava supply stopped and the roof of a lava tube caved in. Hadley Rille, on the edge of one such plain, was visited and studied at close range in 1971 by the crew of Apollo 15 specifically because it offered a rare chance to examine a lunar valley formed by genuine flow rather than by impact, the two great shaping forces, volcanism and bombardment, that between them explain almost everything visible on the lunar surface.

This piece is printed on 100% cotton rag paper, one of a limited edition of ten, each floated and lifted individually so that no two pulls carry an identical wave. It is mounted on a high quality round window-cut mount board, offered in a choice of mount colours, with an acid-free card backing board keeping the paper stable over years. No frame is included, leaving both the frame and the glazing to the buyer's judgement; UV-filtering glass or acrylic is a sensible choice for any wall that receives direct sun for part of the day.

A wave pattern has directionality built into it, a sense of flow across the sheet, and it is worth hanging with that flow in mind relative to the room, letting the movement run parallel to a mantelpiece or shelf line rather than fighting against the room's other horizontals. It suits a wall with some visual quiet around it, where the movement in the print has room to read clearly rather than competing with a busy, patterned space.
