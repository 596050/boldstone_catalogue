# The Fourth Kingdom Nobody Used to Count

This print's four subjects, an animal, a plant, a mineral and a fungus, sound at first like a simple list, but the grouping actually tracks a real and fairly recent argument in the history of science over how nature should be divided up in the first place. For a long time, the answer was three, not four.

Carl Linnaeus, working through the eighteenth century, organised the whole of nature into three kingdoms in his Systema Naturae: Animalia, Vegetabilia and Mineralia, animal, plant and mineral, a scheme intended to cover literally everything in the natural world, living or not. Fungi caused problems within that system almost from the start. They do not photosynthesise as plants do, they do not move or ingest food as animals do, and their basic cell structure and life cycle sit apart from both in ways that were not properly understood until well into the twentieth century, once microscopy and biochemistry advanced enough to show that fungi share more in common with animals at a cellular level than with plants, despite the passive, rooted look that had always grouped them with vegetation by default. Fungi were formally split out into their own kingdom only in the latter half of the twentieth century, once that biochemical evidence became hard to ignore, making the four-part division this print draws from, animal, plant, mineral and now fungus as something distinct from all three, a genuinely modern way of sorting the natural world rather than an old, settled one.

A mineral's presence in that list is worth a second look too, since it is the one subject in the group that was never alive at all, and its inclusion says something about how natural history collecting has always worked in practice rather than in strict taxonomic theory: a collector's cabinet, then and often now, was organised around what came from the earth, sea and air broadly, not around a strict biological definition of "living thing", which is exactly the older, more generous sense of natural history this print's mixed subject matter revives.
