# The Fruit That Takes a Year to Trust

Most people who grow Monstera deliciosa know it only as a houseplant, prized in Britain for those glossy, perforated leaves that make it instantly recognisable on a windowsill. Almost nobody in a temperate climate ever sees it fruit. That is not a design flaw in the plant; it is a matter of conditions. Monstera is a native of the tropical forests of southern Mexico and Central America, a climber that scrambles up tree trunks using aerial roots, and it needs something close to that warmth and humidity to flower and then hold a fruit through the year or more it takes to ripen. Gauteng, South Africa's highveld heartland around Johannesburg and Pretoria, offers exactly the kind of warm, bright garden climate that lets the plant behave outdoors the way it would in the wild — which is presumably how this photograph came to exist at all.

The fruit itself is the strange part. It forms as a green, cone-shaped structure covered in hexagonal scales, closer in outline to a slim corn cob than to anything most people would call a fruit. It takes twelve to fourteen months to ripen fully, and patience is not optional: eaten too early, the flesh is loaded with needle-shaped calcium oxalate crystals that irritate the mouth and throat sharply enough to put most people off trying a second bite. Left to ripen properly, the scales lift and begin to fall away on their own, section by section, revealing sweet white flesh that is often described as tasting like a cross between pineapple and banana — which is exactly where the plant's odd common name comes from. "Delicious monster" is not marketing language; it is a real, long-used nickname for Monstera deliciosa, built from its scientific name and the reward hidden inside a fruit that looks, from the outside, faintly threatening.

The leaves get less credit than they deserve alongside all this. Young Monstera foliage is solid and heart-shaped; only as the plant matures do the familiar splits and holes appear, and botanists still debate exactly why. The leading theories involve light: the holes may let dappled sunlight reach leaves lower down the plant, or they may simply reduce the sail-like area of each leaf enough to stop it tearing in a tropical downpour or gust. Whatever the mechanism, the fenestrated leaf has become one of the most widely reproduced plant shapes in modern design, printed on everything from wallpaper to fabric, largely on the strength of a silhouette that reads instantly even in outline.
