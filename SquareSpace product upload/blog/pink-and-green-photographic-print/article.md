# Why Pink Is the Harder Colour to Photograph

Orange and red flower colour is largely down to carotenoids, stable, robust pigments that hold their colour well under strong light and do not shift much with the plant's internal chemistry. Pink is a different, more temperamental proposition. It comes mainly from anthocyanins, a family of pigments whose exact shade depends on the pH inside the plant cell that holds them: more acidic conditions tend to push the colour toward red, more alkaline conditions push it toward blue or purple, and pink typically sits somewhere in the unstable middle of that range. It is, chemically, a less fixed colour than orange, which is part of why a pink flower can look subtly different depending on the light falling on it, in a way an orange bloom generally does not.

That instability is exactly what this print is working with: an exotic flower in soft pink, held against the cooler green of its own foliage, photographed in light gentle enough to hold the colour's true tone rather than letting it either wash out to a pale, characterless blush or deepen into something closer to red. Pastel colour generally needs softer, more diffused light than a bold primary does. Strong direct sun tends to blow out the highlights on a pale petal long before it does the same to a saturated orange or red one, losing exactly the subtlety of tone that makes a pink flower worth photographing in the first place. The light here is doing careful, quiet work rather than dramatic work, and it shows in how much variation survives across a single petal, from its palest edge to the slightly deeper pink near the flower's centre.

Depth of field carries the rest of the composition. The bloom sits in sharp focus, close enough that the texture of the petal surface, the faint sheen where light catches it, the almost translucent quality at the thinnest edges, is genuinely visible, while the green stem and foliage behind fall away into soft, unfocused colour. That contrast between one sharp subject and a dissolved background is what stops a botanical photograph reading as documentation and lets it read instead as a considered image: a single flower, isolated from the plant it grew on, given room to be looked at properly.

Exotic and tropical flowers of this kind tend to favour bold, simplified petal shapes over the more layered, ruffled forms bred into many temperate garden flowers, an adaptation, generally, to a specific pollinator and to growing conditions where a flower needs to be visible and legible at a distance rather than rewarding only close inspection. Photographed this close, though, that apparent simplicity gives way to real structural detail: the precise point where petal meets stem, the slight asymmetry that no two individual blooms share exactly.

The print itself is produced on aluminium dibond with a matt finish, sized at 60cm x 40cm. Dibond's rigidity means the panel hangs perfectly flat with no risk of the bowing or cockling that can affect a large paper print over time, and the matt surface keeps reflected light from the room from lightening or distorting the pink's more delicate tones, a real consideration for a colour this sensitive to begin with. It suits a wall with soft, indirect natural light rather than one facing directly into strong sun, where the subtlety in the print would otherwise be lost. Pink and green together read as gentle rather than bold, which makes this a good choice for a bedroom, a landing, or any room where you want colour without volume.
