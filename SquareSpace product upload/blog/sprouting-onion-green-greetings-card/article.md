# The Geometry That Was Already There

Slice an onion in half and the first thing you see is not a vegetable so much as a diagram: a set of concentric rings, near-perfect arcs nested one inside another, smallest at the centre and largest at the outer edge. It is one of the most quietly satisfying pieces of geometry in any kitchen, and this card, rather than looking away from the cut bulb toward the fresh green shoot alone, keeps both in view — because the two are not separate subjects. The shoot is simply the innermost ring, still growing.

Each of those rings is not a year of growth, whatever the visual resemblance to a tree stump might suggest. An onion is not woody and it does not lay down one ring per year; a bulb is a highly compressed underground stem, and each ring is the swollen, fleshy base of a single leaf the plant produced during its one growing season, wrapped around the leaf before it and wrapping the leaf after it. Count the rings and you are counting leaves, not years — a record of exactly how much foliage the plant managed to grow before it was lifted from the ground, all of it folded down into concentric storage rather than left standing upright in the usual way a leafy plant displays its work.

That nesting arrangement is also, quite literally, where the future is kept. At the dead centre of all those rings sits the bud — the growing point, already formed, already alive, simply waiting. When the conditions are right, warmth chiefly, that central point does not need to build anything new from scratch. It only needs to keep doing what every ring around it already did once: extend upward and outward. A sprouting onion is not producing some new organ from nowhere. It is the innermost, youngest ring of the same nested structure finally getting the chance to keep going past the point where all the rings before it stopped. The geometry contains its own next step, built in from the start.

There is a pleasing logic, too, in how that plays out visually against the cut cross-section. The rings read as clean, cool arcs — pale, faintly striated, geometrically regular — while the green shoot breaking free of the top is the only element in the whole composition that is not contained by a curve. It is the one part of the onion actually reaching somewhere, set against a structure that is otherwise entirely self-referential, each ring simply repeating the shape of the one inside it.

Watercolour is well suited to holding both of these registers in one image — the precise, almost architectural regularity of the rings, and the looser, more organic form of the growing shoot — because a wash can be controlled tightly enough to describe a clean arc, and loosened just as easily to let a patch of green bleed and soften at its edges the way real new growth does. The discipline of the rings and the freedom of the shoot sit side by side on the same page without either one looking like a compromise.

It is a small reminder that most growth is not really sudden. It only looks that way from outside. Underneath, the structure for what comes next has usually been sitting there, nested and ready, for longer than it appears.
