# What an Onion Looks Like When It Is Allowed to Flower

Most onions never get this far. They are lifted, dried, netted and eaten long before the bulb remembers it is also a plant with somewhere to go. Leave one at the back of a cupboard, though, and it will eventually put out a pale shoot, then a stiff hollow stem, then, if you let it run all the way to the end of its purpose, a flower. That flower is the subject of this painting: a small, dense sphere of star-shaped petals in pink, rising on its stalk the way it would in an allotment bed left to bolt.

This is one of a series of Painted World paintings, each a close study of an ordinary natural subject rendered at a scale that asks you to look properly. Onions, garlic, chives and the ornamental alliums of a spring border all belong to the same genus, and they all flower the same way: not as a single bloom but as an umbel, dozens of tiny six-petalled florets packed into one round head, so that from a distance it reads as a single pink puff and close up it resolves into an entire small society of individual flowers, each on its own short stalk radiating from a common point. It is one of the more satisfying pieces of design in the plant world, the kind of repeating, radiating structure that turns up again and again in nature, from seed heads to shells, and that makes alliums a favourite of garden designers who want a strong sculptural shape without much fuss.

The painting itself is watercolour and inks on 100% cotton rag paper, a heavy, textured surface with enough tooth to hold pigment where you want it and enough absorbency to let a wash bloom and settle unevenly at its edges, the soft, slightly unpredictable bleed that a botanical subject like this benefits from, since real petals are never quite uniform either. Ink is used for definition: the fine dark line that separates one petal from the next, the vein down a leaf, the point where stem meets flower. Watercolour alone would blur those edges; ink alone would flatten the colour. Together they do what the plant itself does, which is combine soft colour with a very exact structure underneath it.

It is mounted on a round window-cut mount board, a circular aperture rather than the usual rectangle, which is a deliberate choice for a subject this symmetrical. A circle draws the eye straight to the centre of the umbel and holds it there, rather than letting it wander to the corners the way a square mount would. It is a device with a long history in botanical illustration, where circular vignettes were used to isolate a single specimen from any distracting background.
