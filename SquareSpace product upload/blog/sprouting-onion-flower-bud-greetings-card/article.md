# When an Onion Decides to Flower

Most sprouting onions in a kitchen do the same modest thing: they send up a green leaf shoot, using stored reserves to keep growing until someone finally uses them or throws them out. This card shows a different, less common event. What is emerging here is not a leaf but a flower bud, still sheathed in its papery covering, riding on top of a thick hollow stalk — the sign of an onion that has switched from vegetative growth to reproduction entirely.

That switch has a name and a trigger. Onions and their relatives are biennial by nature, built to spend one full growing season simply bulking up a bulb, then to pass through a period of cold, and only then, in the following season, to bolt — sending up a flowering stem instead of, or in addition to, ordinary leaf growth. Gardeners and growers generally treat bolting as a nuisance, since a bulb that has diverted its energy into flowering has effectively decided its job is now reproduction rather than storage, and the crop is usually considered spent. But the same reflex that ruins a stored onion for the kitchen is the reflex responsible for one of the most recognisable flower shapes in the whole allium family: a tight bud that will open into a rounded, star-studded globe of many small individual flowers held on stiff, radiating stalks, all fed from that single thick stem.

It is worth pausing on that globe shape, because it is exactly the feature that turned this humble, often unwanted event into a deliberately cultivated one elsewhere in the same plant family. Ornamental alliums — garden varieties bred and selected purely for their flower heads, with names like Allium giganteum and Allium christophii — are prized in borders precisely for this spherical umbel: dozens of tiny star-shaped flowers radiating from a single point on top of a bare, leafless stalk, reading from a distance as one clean geometric ball of colour held above the surrounding planting. Garden designers use them as punctuation, dotted through a border to give height and a repeating round shape without the visual weight of foliage. The unwelcome flower stalk on a bolting kitchen onion and the deliberately grown, much-admired flower head of an ornamental allium are, structurally, the same event: a bulb switching from leaf-making to flower-making, and building the same kind of radiating globe to do it.

Painting the bud at this stage — still closed, still wrapped in its protective papery sheath, the individual florets inside not yet visible — captures the moment just before that transformation is legible. It is a portrait of potential rather than of the finished flower: everything the eventual globe will be is already present, folded tightly inside a covering that has not yet let go. Watercolour handles that hidden stage well, since the sheath itself is thin and slightly translucent, and a careful wash can suggest the crowded structure waiting underneath without resolving it into finished detail — a promise rather than a description.

There is something worth sitting with in an onion that, mid-sprout in a kitchen drawer, quietly commits to becoming a flower instead of simply feeding a meal. It did not ask permission and it will not be talked out of it. Given the cold spell and the warmth that followed, the plant has made its decision, and the bud pushing up through the papery sheath is that decision becoming visible.
