# A Vegetable That Was Always Waiting

Left in a dark cupboard for long enough, an onion will eventually do something that looks, to most people, like spoiling: it softens slightly, and a pale green shoot pushes up out of its top, sometimes forcing its way between the papery outer layers before it ever reaches open air. It is not spoiling. It is the plant doing precisely what a bulb is built to do, and this print, one of five botanical studies following that process, treats the moment as what it actually is: a stored plant resuming a life cycle that was only ever paused.

A bulb is, in essence, a survival strategy, a compact underground store of food and moisture built up over one growing season so the plant can sit out an unfavourable period, cold, drought, or simply darkness, and then resume growth quickly once conditions improve, without needing to start again from a seed. Every layer inside an onion is a swollen leaf base packed with stored carbohydrate, arranged around a central growing point, or apical bud, that has been sitting dormant at the centre the whole time, held in check by the same internal chemical signals, principally the hormone auxin, that keep a tree's side buds suppressed while its main shoot grows on. Once those suppressing signals weaken, whether from age, warmth, or simply enough time passing in storage, the apical bud reactivates and begins drawing on the layers around it as fuel, converting stored starches into the sugars needed to build new leaf tissue fast.

What follows is a race toward light that most people never watch happen. Growing in the dark of a cupboard, the new shoot etiolates: it grows pale, yellow-white rather than green, and stretches unusually long and thin, putting its limited energy reserve entirely into reaching upward rather than into producing the chlorophyll it does not yet need, since chlorophyll production in most plants requires light to trigger it. Only once the shoot breaks into daylight does it typically green up and thicken, redirecting its growth from a stretching, light-seeking mode into a photosynthesising one. A sprouting onion on a kitchen counter is, in other words, a plant visibly switching survival strategies in real time, from stored reserve to active grower, which is a slower and genuinely more interesting event than the word "sprouting" usually gets credit for.
