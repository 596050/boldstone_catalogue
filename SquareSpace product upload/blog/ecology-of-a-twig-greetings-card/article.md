# A Whole Habitat, an Inch Wide

Strip a tree back to a single bare twig in winter and there is, against expectation, still a great deal to look at. A terminal bud sits at the tip holding next spring's leaves already folded and waiting, packed in miniature inside protective scales built to shrug off frost. Along the twig's length, small raised marks called lenticels let the living tissue beneath the bark breathe, doing quietly for a stem what stomata do for a leaf. Pale crescent-shaped scars mark exactly where last year's leaves let go each in the same place, and older ring-like scars further down record every bud break the twig has managed since it was new growth two, three, four winters ago. None of this requires a microscope. It only requires stopping at a single twig for longer than most people ever do.

Foresters and botanists have long used exactly these features to identify deciduous trees in winter, when the leaves that would otherwise make the job simple are absent. Bud arrangement, whether opposite or alternate along the stem, bud shape, twig colour, the pattern of leaf scars and the pith visible in a cut cross-section together form a kind of fingerprint, specific enough that a trained eye can name a species from a single leafless twig held in the hand. It is a discipline built entirely on the idea that a small, apparently featureless piece of a tree is, in fact, dense with identifying detail, if looked at with the right kind of attention.

A twig is also, in the most literal sense, a habitat rather than merely a structure. Lichen colonises bark that stays undisturbed long enough. Insects lay eggs in the shelter of a bud scale or a fork in the wood, timed to hatch as the spring's new growth becomes available to feed on. Moss can gain a foothold in a shaded crook where bark holds moisture longest. A single twig, in other words, is rarely just plant tissue; it is plant tissue supporting a small, specific community of other organisms that depend on exactly that particular twig's position, shelter and timing.

This drawing takes that idea at face value — treating one ordinary twig with the same close attention usually reserved for a whole tree or a dramatic flower, on the reasoning that the smaller the subject, the more a patient eye tends to find in it. It is a fitting subject for graphite, a medium built for exactly this kind of unhurried, detailed attention rather than for broad, quick colour.

Sent as a note, it suits anyone who prefers modest, overlooked subjects to showy ones — a card for a naturalist, a forester, or simply someone who has never quite looked at a bare winter branch this closely before.
