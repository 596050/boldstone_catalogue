# A Surface That Is Darker Than It Looks

The ring-within-ring pattern in this piece comes from one of the most basic events in marbling: a drop of colour hits the floating surface of the size bath and, rather than sinking, spreads outward evenly in every direction until it meets resistance, either from the edge of the tray or from another ring of colour already spreading nearby, at which point the two rings push against one another and stop, leaving a visible seam. Add a drop of clear surfactant, traditionally ox gall, into the centre of an existing ring and it does the opposite: it repels the colour outward from that single point, hollowing out a pale or white centre and leaving a bubble, a ring with nothing at its heart. Chains of these bubbles, one repelling into the next, build the branching, cellular field that gives this piece its structure and its name.

It is a reasonable assumption that the moon, seen bright against a night sky, must be reflecting most of the sunlight that reaches it. It is not. The lunar surface is covered in dark, fine-grained volcanic rock and dust, and reflects on average only around twelve per cent of the sunlight falling on it, a reflectivity, or albedo, roughly comparable to worn asphalt or a lump of coal, far lower than most people would guess. The moon looks bright for two reasons that have nothing to do with how reflective it actually is: it is being lit by direct, unfiltered sunlight, and it is being viewed against the almost total blackness of space or a dark night sky, a contrast so extreme that even a dim, coal-coloured surface reads to the human eye as dazzlingly white. Photographs taken from the lunar surface itself, alongside genuinely white objects like an astronaut's suit, make the discrepancy obvious: next to true white, the ground the astronauts are standing on looks distinctly grey.
