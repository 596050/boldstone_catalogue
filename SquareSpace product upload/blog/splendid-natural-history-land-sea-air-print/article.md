# Why Draw It, When a Camera Was Available

Photography has been capable of recording a shell or a piece of coral in exact, reproducible detail for well over a century, and yet observational drawing has never gone away as a tool of natural history, this print among the recent evidence for that. The reason is not sentimental. A drawing, unlike a photograph, is a series of decisions rather than a single captured instant, and every one of those decisions can be aimed deliberately at making a specimen easier to understand rather than simply recording how the light happened to fall on it that day.

A photograph records everything in its frame with equal fidelity: a distracting shadow, a chip in the shell, a reflection off a wet surface, all rendered with exactly as much attention as the diagnostic ridge or spiral that actually identifies the species. A trained illustrator can suppress the shadow, omit the accidental chip, remove the reflection, and instead put slightly more emphasis on the one structural detail, a rib count, the angle of a whorl, the branching pattern of a coral colony, that a specialist actually needs to see clearly. This is precisely why scientific illustration survived, and in some fields still thrives, well into the age of high-resolution photography and digital scanning: herbaria still commission botanical illustrations of type specimens, and entomological plates are still drawn rather than photographed when a publication needs to show, unambiguously, the single feature that separates one species from a near-identical relative. The tradition runs from the expedition draughtsmen who sailed on nineteenth-century voyages of scientific discovery, recording specimens no camera of the day could have captured in useful detail, through to natural history illustration practised today for exactly the same reason: clarity over documentation.
