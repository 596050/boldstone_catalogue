# Falls, Standards and the Blue of an Iris

An iris flower is really two flowers pretending to be one. Look closely and it separates into two distinct sets of petals doing two different jobs: the falls, three broad petals that arch downward and outward, acting as a landing platform and signpost for a pollinating insect, and the standards, three narrower petals that stand upright at the centre, protecting the flower's reproductive parts and giving the bloom its height and structure. Most people see an iris as a single soft shape. A photograph taken close enough shows the architecture: six petals, two postures, one flower doing the work of persuading an insect to land in exactly the right place to pick up pollen on its way through.

This print is that close look, taken at a distance that lets the falls and standards separate from each other properly, with the deep blue-violet of the petals rendered in enough detail to see the fine veining that runs through a fall like ink through wet paper, and, depending on the variety, the raised, textured ridge or beard that runs down the centre of the fall, acting as a landing guide and grip for a visiting bee. Blue and violet colour in iris petals comes from anthocyanin pigments, the same broad family responsible for blue and purple across the plant world, their exact shade shifting with the pH inside the plant's cells, which is part of why iris blue is never quite a flat, uniform colour but a gradient, deeper toward the throat of the flower and lighter at the petal's edge, exactly the kind of gradient a macro photograph can hold onto in a way the eye, moving quickly past a border, generally does not.

It is not a coincidence that iris turns up so often in art. Van Gogh painted an entire bed of them at Saint-Remy in 1889, drawn to precisely this combination of strong structural repetition and rich, saturated colour, a flower that reads as pattern and as individual bloom simultaneously, which is a rare combination and a gift to anyone working in a visual medium, whether oil paint or a photographic lens. The appeal has not dated.

Photographically, the challenge with a flower this dark and this saturated is exposure: blue-violet petals can easily lose all detail into a flat block of colour if the light is too flat or too harsh. Getting the falls and standards to separate as clearly as they do here depends on a softer, more directional light, enough to model the curve of each petal in shadow and highlight, without blowing out the paler markings near the centre of the bloom. That is the difference between a photograph of an iris and a photograph that actually shows you what an iris is doing.
