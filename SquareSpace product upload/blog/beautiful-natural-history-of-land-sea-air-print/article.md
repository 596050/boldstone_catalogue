# One Specimen From Everywhere at Once

Four objects share this composition, and the description behind it is unusually precise about what they are: an animal, a plant, a mineral and a fungus, each one drawn from direct observation and each one, in its own way, once part of the living world, brought together from land, sea and air into a single sheet. It is not a scene. Nothing in it shares a habitat with anything else. What holds the composition together is the act of close, patient looking applied evenly across categories that do not otherwise belong side by side, printed on high quality matt-finish 100% white cotton 310gsm paper in a choice of three sizes.

Bringing unrelated specimens together on one surface has a long history, and it long predates the natural history museum as an institution. Cabinets of curiosities, or Wunderkammer, were assembled across Europe from the sixteenth century onward, private rooms in which a collector displayed shells beside minerals beside dried plants beside taxidermy beside deliberately strange objects with no natural history value at all, organised not by the rigorous taxonomy that would come later but by wonder, rarity and resemblance. A coiled ammonite might sit beside a coiled seashell beside a piece of coral, grouped because the eye saw a family relationship between their forms rather than because any of them were biologically related. That instinct, to group by visual and structural likeness across the conventional boundaries of animal, plant and mineral, is exactly what a composition like this one revives, deliberately, on a single sheet of paper rather than across a room of specimen drawers.

The drawings themselves work at the level of the diagnostic, not the decorative. Observational natural history illustration has always had a job to do beyond looking pleasant: a species is identified by the precise curve of a shell's whorl, the vein pattern of a leaf, the exact branching angle of a coral colony, details a general impression cannot supply but a trained, close drawing can. Printed here on 310gsm cotton paper with a matt finish, that fine linework holds without the sheen or glare a glossy paper would introduce, keeping the detail legible from a normal viewing distance rather than only under raking light.

The print comes in three sizes, A4 at 297mm x 210mm, A3 at 420mm x 297mm and A2 at 594mm x 420mm, each one exactly double the paper area of the size before it under the standard ISO system, so moving up a size is a genuine doubling of scale rather than a marginal step. A4 suits a grouped, gallery-style arrangement of several prints at close range; A2 is large enough to act as a single, standalone piece from across a room. Each comes with an acid-free card backing board rather than a frame, so the size and style of frame is entirely up to the room it will hang in.

Given its mixed, almost encyclopaedic subject matter, this piece suits a room that rewards a second and third look: a dining room wall, a study, a wide hallway where guests pause rather than pass straight through. Keep it out of direct, prolonged sun and glaze with UV-filtering glass, since fine pigment line is often the first thing to soften under strong light.
