# The Maths in a Spiky Rosette

Look at an agave from directly above, which is how this photograph seems to have been taken, and the plant stops looking like a collection of leaves and starts looking like a diagram. Each blade grows from the same low point and fans outward in a spiral, angled so that no leaf sits directly above the one below it. That is not decorative choice. It is the plant solving a problem: how to give every leaf its share of sunlight without the leaves above shading out the leaves beneath. Botanists call the arrangement phyllotaxis, and the angles involved tend to follow the same proportions found in sunflower heads, pine cones and pineapples — an efficient packing rule that nature keeps rediscovering because it works, not because it is pretty, though it is that too.

Agave is a plant of the Americas — Mexico chiefly, with relatives scattered through the arid southern United States and Central America — which makes its presence in a Zimbabwean setting a matter of travel rather than origin. It has moved well beyond its native range over the past two centuries, prized wherever gardeners want structure without irrigation: a plant that stores water in thick, waxy leaves and shrugs off drought that would finish off most ornamentals. In parts of rural Africa the larger agave species and their sisal relatives are grown deliberately as living boundary hedges, their marginal teeth and terminal spines making a fence that needs no maintenance and no wire. The same spines that look sculptural in a photograph are, from the plant's point of view, armour against anything that might otherwise eat it.

The species behind the popular name "century plant" — a title that has attached itself loosely to several large agaves — spends ten, twenty, sometimes thirty years doing nothing more dramatic than holding its rosette, before sending up a flowering stalk that can reach several metres in a matter of weeks, using reserves the plant has been quietly banking the entire time. It flowers once. Then it dies, the whole rosette having existed, in effect, to produce a single event. Few garden plants commit so completely to one performance.

None of that drama is what this card shows, and it does not need it: the leaf forms alone carry the interest, that spiral geometry photographed close enough that the arrangement, not the individual leaf, becomes the subject. It is a good example of a habit worth noticing in Boldstone's wider work as well as in its cards — the way an ordinary structural fact about a living thing, examined closely enough, starts to look like design.
