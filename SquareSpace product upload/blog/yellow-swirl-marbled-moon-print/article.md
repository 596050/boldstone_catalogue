# The Bright Loops Nobody Can Fully Explain

A single tine, drawn once through a bath of floating colour rings in a loose spiral, is enough to make a swirl pattern, the marbling equivalent of the "stone" pattern that gives the whole craft its name in several languages. The tool barely disturbs the surface, dragging the outer edge of each ring into the next without breaking them apart entirely, so the finished pattern keeps a memory of the original circles even as it is pulled into long, looping bands. It is a technique that rewards restraint: too many passes and the distinct rings collapse into an undifferentiated smear, so the swirl in a finished piece like this one is usually the record of a single, confident movement rather than a laboured one.

The moon carries its own bright, looping swirls, and they are among the stranger unsolved puzzles in lunar science. Lunar swirls are pale, sinuous markings on the surface, the best known called Reiner Gamma, that look almost painted onto the dark volcanic plains rather than formed by any of the usual cratering or lava processes, since they have no measurable relief at all, no raised or lowered ground, just a marked difference in brightness. What they do coincide with, closely, is small patches of localised magnetic field, tiny compared with Earth's global field but strong enough, it is thought, to deflect part of the stream of charged particles arriving from the sun, the solar wind, that otherwise slowly darkens exposed lunar soil over time through a process called space weathering. Where the local field shields the surface, the theory goes, that darkening happens more slowly or not at all, leaving a bright swirl behind that traces the shape of the invisible magnetic patch beneath it. It remains one of the moon's genuinely open questions, studied precisely because the mechanism, while plausible, has never been fully confirmed.
